The rate of '''participation of women in the Olympic Games''' has been increasing since their first participation in [[1900 Summer Olympics|1900]]. Some disciplines are uniquely for women, others are contested by both sexes, while some older disciplines remain for men only. Studies of media coverage of the Olympics consistently show differences in the ways in which women and men are described and the ways in which their performances are discussed. As of 2023, 41.1% of members are women. The 2024 Paris Olympics were  the first to have the goal of achieving gender parity between men and women.<ref>{{Cite web |date=2018-02-06 |title=The IOC takes historic step forward to advance gender equality following Executive Board approval of bold recommendations |url=https://olympics.com/ioc/news/the-ioc-takes-historic-step-forward-to-advance-gender-equality-following-executive-board-approval-of-bold-recommendations |access-date=2024-09-08 |website=International Olympic Committee}}</ref><ref>{{Cite web |date=2014-12-07 |title=Guide to IOC President Thomas Bach's reform plans |url=https://apnews.com/guide-to-ioc-president-thomas-bachs-reform-plans-0620c10bc4a64cb28f6c342fcda9be73 |access-date=2024-09-09 |website=AP News |language=en}}</ref>

== History of women at the Olympics ==
=== 1900 ===
[[File:Charlotte cooper sterry 2.jpg|thumb|left|Charlotte Cooper]]

The first modern Olympic Games to feature female athletes was the [[1900 Summer Olympics|1900 Games]] in Paris.<ref>{{Cite web|url=http://www.laici.va/content/laici/en/sezioni/donna/notizie/le-donne-alle-olimpiadi.html|title=Women at the Olympics|website=Pontifical Council for the Laity|access-date=July 31, 2017}}</ref> [[Hélène de Pourtalès]] of Switzerland became the first woman to compete at the Olympic Games. She was the first female Olympic champion as a member of the winning team in the first [[Sailing at the 1900 Summer Olympics – 1 to 2 ton|1 to 2 ton]] sailing event on May 22, 1900.<ref>{{Cite web|url=http://www.topendsports.com/events/summer/women.htm|title=Women at the Olympic Games|website=topendsports.com|access-date=March 17, 2017}}</ref><ref>{{Cite web|url=http://www.sport-olympic.gr/sp/index.php/olympic-games/modern-olympic-games/summer-olympic-games/1900-paris-summer-olympics/1459-1900-summer-olympics-the-results-sailing|title=1900 Summer Olympics - The Results (Sailing) - Sport-Olympic.com|last=Lygkas|first=Giannis|website=sport-olympic.gr|access-date=March 17, 2017}}</ref> Briton [[Charlotte Cooper (tennis)|Charlotte Cooper]] became the first female individual champion by winning the [[Tennis at the 1900 Summer Olympics – Women's singles|women's singles tennis competition]] on July 11.<ref>{{Cite web|url=https://www.sports-reference.com/olympics/summer/1900/TEN/womens-singles.html|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20200418132610/https://www.sports-reference.com/olympics/summer/1900/TEN/womens-singles.html|url-status=dead|archive-date=April 18, 2020|title=Tennis at the 1900 Paris Summer Games: Women's Singles|website=Olympics at Sports-Reference.com|access-date=March 17, 2017}}</ref> Tennis and golf were the only sports where women could compete in individual disciplines. 22 women competed at the 1900 Games, 2.2% of all the competitors.<ref name=":0">{{Cite web|url=https://stillmed.olympic.org/Documents/Reference_documents_Factsheets/The_Olympic_Summer_Games.pdf|title=Factsheet – The Games of the Olympiad|date=October 28, 2013|publisher=International Olympic Committee|access-date=March 17, 2017|archive-date=April 22, 2020|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20200422134610/https://stillmed.olympic.org/Documents/Reference_documents_Factsheets/The_Olympic_Summer_Games.pdf|url-status=dead}}</ref> Alongside sailing, golf and tennis, women also competed in [[croquet]].

[[File:Margaret Abbott golf event 1900 Olympic Games.png|thumb|Margaret Abbott plays in the 1900 Olympic Games women's golf event in Compiegne, France.]]
There were several firsts in the women's golf. This was the first time ever that women competed in the Olympic Games. The women's division was won by [[Margaret Abbott]] of [[Chicago Golf Club]]. Abbott shot a 47 to win and became the first ever American female to win a [[gold medal]] in the Olympic Games,<ref>{{Cite web | url=https://www.olympic.org/margaret-abbott | title=Margaret ABBOTT - Olympic Golf &#124; United States of America| date=2017-02-16}}</ref> though she received a gilded porcelain bowl as a prize instead of a medal. She is also the second overall American woman to receive an Olympic medal. Abbott's mother, [[Mary Abbott (golfer)|Mary Abbott]], also competed in this Olympic event and finished tied for seventh, shooting a 65. They were the first and only mother and daughter that have ever competed in the same Olympic event at the same time.<ref>{{Cite web | url=https://www.atlasobscura.com/articles/the-first-american-woman-to-win-an-olympic-championship-didnt-even-know-it | title=Margaret Abbott Won an Olympic Medal in 1900, but Never Found Out| date=2016-08-10}}</ref> Margaret never knew that they were competing in the Olympics; she thought it was a normal golf tournament and died not knowing. Her historic victory was not known until [[University of Florida]] professor Paula Welch began to do research into the history of the Olympics and discovered that Margaret Abbott had placed first. Over the course of ten years, she contacted Abbott's children and informed them of their mother's victory.<ref>{{Cite web | url=https://www.teamusa.org/News/2016/March/21/Margaret-Abbott-Aced-Team-USAs-First-Womens-Olympic-Gold-Medal-And-Didnt-Know-It | archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20160324084347/http://www.teamusa.org/News/2016/March/21/Margaret-Abbott-Aced-Team-USAs-First-Womens-Olympic-Gold-Medal-And-Didnt-Know-It | url-status=dead | archive-date=March 24, 2016 | title=Margaret Abbott Aced Team USA's First Women's Olympic Gold Medal and Didn't Know It}}</ref><ref>{{Cite web | url=http://www.womengolfersmuseum.com/Famousgolfers/AbbottMargaret.htm | title=Women Golfers' Museum}}</ref>

Jane Moulin and [[Elvira Guerra]] competed in the [[Equestrian at the 1900 Summer Olympics – Hacks and hunter combined|hacks and hunter combined (''chevaux de selle'')]] equestrian event at the 1900 Games. Originally only the jumping equestrian events were counted as "Olympic", but IOC records later added the hacks and hunter and [[mail coach]] races to the official list of 1900 events, retroactively making Moulin and Guerra among the first female Olympians.<ref name="cite">{{cite web|title= Paris 1900|publisher= International Olympic Committee |archive-date=2016-06-02|url-status= live| url=https://www.olympic.org/paris-1900| access-date=2017-10-02|archive-url= https://web.archive.org/web/20160602113114/https://www.olympic.org/paris-1900}}</ref><ref name="Mallon">{{cite book |url=https://books.google.com/books?id=inAwCgAAQBAJ&pg=PA25 | author = Mallon, Bill | year = 1998 | title = The 1900 Olympic Games, Results for All Competitors in All Events, with Commentary | publisher = McFarland & Company, Inc. | location = Jefferson, North Carolina | isbn = 978-0-7864-4064-1}}</ref>

=== 1904–1916 ===
[[File:Queenie Newall.jpg|thumb|[[Queenie Newall]] competed in archery at the 1908 London Games.]]

In [[1904 Summer Olympics|1904]], the women's [[Archery at the 1904 Summer Olympics|archery]] event was added, marking a significant step in including women in Olympic competitions.<ref>{{Cite web|url=https://www.sports-reference.com/olympics/summer/1904/ARC/|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20200417050133/https://www.sports-reference.com/olympics/summer/1904/ARC/|url-status=dead|archive-date=April 17, 2020|title=Archery at the 1904 St. Louis Summer Games|website=Sports-Reference.com|access-date=July 31, 2017}}</ref><ref>{{Cite news|url=http://www.history.com/news/8-unusual-facts-about-the-1904-st-louis-olympics|title=8 Unusual Facts About the 1904 St. Louis Olympics – History in the Headlines|last=Andrews|first=Evan|date=August 29, 2014|work=HISTORY.com|access-date=July 31, 2017}}</ref>

By the time of the London 1908 Olympics, there were 37 female athletes who competed in archery, tennis, and figure skating. This represented a growing but still limited participation of women in the Games.<ref name=":0"/>

Moving forward to [[1912 Summer Olympics|Stockholm 1912]], the number of women participating increased to 47, and new sports such as [[Swimming at the 1912 Summer Olympics|swimming]] and [[Diving at the 1912 Summer Olympics – Women's 10 metre platform|diving]] were introduced. However, figure skating and archery were removed from the program for that year. Despite these changes, Stockholm 1912 included [[Art competitions at the Summer Olympics|art competitions]] that were open to women, although detailed records of their participation were not consistently kept. The progression in women's participation up to [[1912 Summer Olympics|Stockholm 1912]] shows a gradual expansion in both the number of sports and athletes involved, though it was still far from the comprehensive inclusion we see in modern Olympic Games. These early years laid foundational steps for women's sports in the Olympics, reflecting societal shifts towards greater gender equity in athletic competition.
 
The [[1916 Summer Olympics]] were due to be held in [[Berlin]] but were cancelled following the outbreak of [[World War I]].<ref>{{Cite news|url=https://nationalpost.com/sports/olympics/the-games-that-never-were-how-germany-almost-hosted-the-1916-olympics-in-the-middle-of-world-war|title=The Games that never were: How Germany almost hosted the 1916 Olympics — in the middle of a war|last=Faris|first=Nick|date=August 3, 2016|work=National Post|access-date=January 16, 2017}}</ref>

=== 1920–1928 ===
[[File:Bundesarchiv_Bild_102-11014,_Jaross_Szabo.jpg|left|thumb|[[Herma Szabo]] was an Austrian [[figure skater]] who made history as the first woman to win an Olympic gold medal in figure skating 1924.]]

In 1920, 65 women competed at the Games. [[Archery at the 1920 Summer Olympics|Archery]] was added back into the programme.

A record 135 female athletes competed at [[1924 Summer Olympics|Paris 1924]]. [[Fencing at the 1924 Summer Olympics – Women's foil|Women's fencing]] made its debut with Dane [[Ellen Osiier]] winning the inaugural gold.<ref>{{Cite web|url=http://users.skynet.be/hermandw/olymp/family.html|title=Olympic History for Families|date=February 10, 2000|website=users.skynet.be|access-date=August 2, 2017|edition=December 22, 2000|archive-date=March 4, 2016|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20160304111037/http://users.skynet.be/hermandw/olymp/family.html|url-status=dead}}</ref> Archery was again removed from the programme of sports. [[Dorothy Margaret Stuart]] was the first woman to gain a medal in the [[Art competitions at the Summer Olympics|arts]], winning silver in [[List of Olympic medalists in art competitions#Mixed literature|mixed literature]].<ref>{{cite web|url=https://www.olympedia.org/athletes/920551 |title=Dorothy Margaret Stuart |work=Olympedia |access-date=3 August 2020}}</ref>

In [[1924 Winter Olympics|1924]], the first Winter Olympics also took place, with women competing only in [[Figure skating at the 1924 Winter Olympics|figure skating]]. [[Herma Szabo]] became the first ever female Winter Olympic champion when she won the [[Figure skating at the 1924 Winter Olympics – Ladies' singles|ladies' singles competition]].<ref>{{Cite news|url=https://austria-forum.org/af/AEIOU/Szabo,_Herma|title=Szabo, Herma|last=Walter|first=Pachl|date=August 17, 2009|work=Austria-Forum|access-date=August 1, 2017|edition=March 25, 2016|language=de}}</ref>

[[File:HitomiKinue_at_Ams.jpg|thumb|[[Kinue Hitomi]] won a silver medal in the Women's 800 meters at the 1928 Amsterdam Olympics. The first woman from Asia to win an Olympic medal.]]

At the 1928 Winter Olympics in [[St Moritz]], no changes were made to any female events. Fifteen year old [[Sonja Henie]] won her inaugural of three Olympic gold medals.<ref>{{Cite web|url=https://www.biography.com/people/sonja-henie-9334831|title=Sonja Henie|date=April 2, 2014|website=Biography.com|publisher=A&E Television Networks|access-date=August 2, 2017|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20190324112828/https://www.biography.com/people/sonja-henie-9334831|archive-date=March 24, 2019|url-status=dead}}</ref> This year also marked an increase in women's participation from less than 5% of the total number of athletes in previous years to 10% in 1928.

At the Summer Games of the same year, women's athletics and gymnastics made their debut.<ref>{{Cite web|url=http://faculty.elmira.edu/dmaluso/sports/timeline/gymnastics.html|title=Timeline of Women in Sports|website=faculty.elmira.edu|access-date=August 2, 2017}}</ref> In athletics, women competed in the [[Athletics at the 1928 Summer Olympics – Women's 100 metres|100 metres]], [[Athletics at the 1928 Summer Olympics – Women's 800 metres|800 metres]], [[Athletics at the 1928 Summer Olympics – Women's 4 × 100 metres relay|4 × 100 metres relay]], [[Athletics at the 1928 Summer Olympics – Women's high jump|high jump]] and [[Athletics at the 1928 Summer Olympics – Women's discus throw|discus throw]]. The 800-metre race was won by [[Lina Radke]] (on the right in the photograph) but was controversial as many competitors were reportedly exhausted or unable to complete the race.<ref>{{Cite web|url=http://library.la84.org/SportsLibrary/NASSH_Proceedings/NP1985/NP1985zc.pdf|title=An Examination of The 1928 Olympic 800 Meter Race For Women|last=Emery|first=Lynne|website=la84.org|access-date=February 1, 2018|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20180923092609/http://library.la84.org/SportsLibrary/NASSH_Proceedings/NP1985/NP1985zc.pdf|archive-date=September 23, 2018|url-status=dead}}</ref> Consequently, the IOC decided to drop the 800 metres from the programme; it was not reinstated until 1960.<ref>{{Cite book|title=Women and Sport: Interdisciplinary Perspectives|last1=Guthrie|first1=Sharon Ruth|last2=Costa|first2=D. Margaret|publisher=Human Kinetics|year=1994|isbn=9780873226868|pages=127–128}}</ref> [[Halina Konopacka]] of [[Poland at the 1928 Summer Olympics|Poland]] became the first female Olympic champion in athletics by winning the discus throw.<ref>{{Cite news|url=http://eurosport.onet.pl/lekkoatletyka/halina-konopacka-pierwsza-dama-drugiej-rzeczypospolitej-wciaz-czeka-na-order-orla/4fpvlm|title=Halina Konopacka – pierwsza dama Drugiej Rzeczypospolitej – wciąż czeka na Order Orła Białego|last=Petruczenko|first=Maciej|date=May 17, 2017|work=Onet Sport|access-date=August 2, 2017|language=pl-PL}}</ref> At the [[Gymnastics at the 1928 Summer Olympics – Women's team|gymnastics competition]], the host [[Netherlands at the 1928 Summer Olympics|Dutch team]] won the first gold medal for women in the sport.<ref>{{Cite web|url=https://thegymter.net/2015/12/11/our-first-olympics-womens-gymnastics-at-amsterdam-1928/|title=Our First Olympics: Women's Gymnastics at Amsterdam 1928|last=Price|first=Jessica Taylor|date=December 11, 2012|website=The Gymternet|access-date=August 3, 2017}}</ref> Tennis was removed from the program.

===1932–1936===
The [[1932 Summer Olympics]] in Los Angeles debuted the [[Athletics at the 1932 Summer Olympics – Women's javelin throw|javelin throw]] and the [[Athletics at the 1932 Summer Olympics – Women's 80 metres hurdles|80 metres hurdles]], which expanded the athletic landscape. A significant highlight was [[Babe Didrikson Zaharias]], an American athlete who won two gold medals in the 80 metres hurdles and javelin and a silver medal in the high jump, drawing considerable attention to women's athletics. Four years later, at the [[1936 Winter Olympics|1936 Winter Games]] in Garmisch-Partenkirchen, women competed in the [[alpine skiing]] combined event for the first time, with German athlete [[Christl Cranz]] securing the inaugural gold medal.<ref>{{Cite web|url=http://www.marcolympics.org/jeux/1936/winter/alpin_r.php|title=The Garmisch Partenkirchen 1936 Olympic Winter Games sport result: alpine skiing|last=Boucher|first=Marc|website=marcolympics.org|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20080828030122/http://www.marcolympics.org/jeux/1936/winter/alpin_r.php|archive-date=2008-08-28|url-status=dead|access-date=August 4, 2017}}</ref> The [[1936 Summer Olympics]] in Berlin reintroduced gymnastics to the women's programme.

=== 1940–1944 ===
The [[1940 Winter Olympics]] due to be held in Sapporo, the [[1940 Summer Olympics]] due to be held in Tokyo, the [[1944 Winter Olympics]] due to be held in Cortina d'Ampezzo and the [[1944 Summer Olympics]] due to be held in London were all cancelled due to the outbreak of [[World War II]]. === 1948–1956 ===
[[File:Larisa_Latynina_1964.jpg|thumb|[[Larisa Latynina]] during the vault award ceremony at the 1964 Olympics.]]
At the [[1948 Winter Olympics]] in St. Moritz, women made their debut in the [[Alpine skiing at the 1948 Winter Olympics – Women's downhill|downhill]] and [[Alpine skiing at the 1948 Winter Olympics – Women's slalom|slalom]] events, having only competed in the [[Alpine skiing at the 1936 Winter Olympics – Women's combined|combined event in 1936]]. The women therefore competed in all of the same alpine skiing events as the men. [[Barbara Ann Scott]] of [[Canada at the 1948 Winter Olympics|Canada]] won the [[Figure skating at the 1948 Winter Olympics|ladies' singles figure skating competition]], marking the first time a non-European won the gold medal in the event.<ref>{{Cite news|url=http://www.thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/en/article/barbara-ann-scott/|title=Barbara Ann Scott|last=Schrodt|first=Barbara|date=September 30, 2012|work=The Canadian Encyclopedia|access-date=August 4, 2017|edition=March 4, 2015}}</ref> At the London [[1948 Summer Olympics]], women competed in canoeing for the first time.<ref name=":1">{{Cite news|url=http://www.nbcolympics.com/news/canoe-kayak-101-olympic-history|title=Canoe/Kayak 101: Olympic history|last=Lopez|first=Cesar|date=March 30, 2016|work=NBC Olympics|access-date=August 5, 2017|archive-date=August 6, 2018|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20180806030650/http://www.nbcolympics.com/news/canoe-kayak-101-olympic-history|url-status=dead}}</ref> The women competed in the [[Canoeing at the 1948 Summer Olympics – Women's K-1 500 metres|K-1 500 metres event]].<ref name=":1" /> [[Alice Coachman]] won a gold medal in the women's high jump at the [[1948 Summer Olympics]], marking the first gold medal won by a Black woman for the United States. At the [[1952 Winter Olympics]] held in Oslo, women competed in [[Cross-country skiing at the 1952 Winter Olympics|cross-country skiing]] for the first time. They competed in the [[Cross-country skiing at the 1952 Winter Olympics – Women's 10 km|10 kilometre distance]]. At the [[1952 Summer Olympics]] held in Helsinki, women were allowed to compete in [[Equestrian at the 1952 Summer Olympics|equestrian]] for the first time since the [[Equestrian at the 1900 Summer Olympics – Hacks and hunter combined|hacks and hunter combined event in 1900]].<ref name=":2">{{Cite news|url=https://www.horse-canada.com/horses-and-history/unique-bizarre-and-memorable-olympic-equestrian-moments-p1/|title=Unique, Bizarre and Memorable Olympic Equestrian Moments – Part I|date=August 5, 2012|work=Horse Canada|access-date=August 5, 2017}}</ref> They competed in the dressage event which was open to both men and women to compete against one another.<ref name=":2" /> Danish equestrian [[Lis Hartel]] of Denmark won the silver medal in the [[Equestrian at the 1952 Summer Olympics – Individual dressage|individual competition]] alongside men.<ref>{{Cite web|url=http://www.olympiandatabase.com/index.php?id=10698&L=1|title=Dressage individuel Mixed – Equestrian at the 1952 Summer Olympics in Helsinki – Results|website=olympiandatabase.com|publisher=Sportsencylco|access-date=August 5, 2017}}</ref> At the [[1956 Winter Olympics]] held in Cortina d'Ampezzo, the [[Cross-country skiing at the 1956 Winter Olympics – Ladies' 3 × 5 kilometre relay|3 × 5 kilometre cross-country relay event]] was added to the programme. The [[1956 Summer Olympics]] held in Melbourne had a programme identical to that of the prior Olympiad.<ref group="note">The [[Equestrian at the 1956 Summer Olympics|equestrian events]] for these Games were held in Stockholm due to Australia's strict equine quarantine laws.</ref>

[[File:Sata Isobe 1964.jpg|thumb|left|The [[Oriental Witches]], a name given by the reports of European media given their victories.<ref name="kotobank">[https://kotobank.jp/word/東洋の魔女-672604 Oriental Witches ｜kotobank]{{in lang|ja}}</ref>]]

=== 1972–1980 ===
[[File:Nadia Comăneci Moscow1980c.jpg|thumb|upright|[[Nadia Comăneci]] on the [[balance beam]], 1980.]]
At the [[1972 Winter Olympics]] held in Sapporo there were no changes to the sports open to women. At the [[1972 Summer Olympics]] in Munich, [[Archery at the 1972 Summer Olympics – Women's individual|archery]] was held for the first time since 1920.<ref>{{Cite web|url=http://www.softschools.com/facts/sports/archery_facts/787/|title=Archery Facts|website=softschools.com|access-date=September 20, 2017}}</ref> At the [[1976 Winter Olympics]] in Innsbruck, ice dancing was added to the programme.<ref group="note">Ice dancing is a pairs event with one male and one female.</ref> Women competed in three new events at the [[1976 Summer Olympics]] held in Montreal. Women debuted in [[Basketball at the 1976 Summer Olympics|basketball]] and [[Handball at the 1976 Summer Olympics|handball]].<ref>{{Cite news|url=https://www.usab.com/history/national-team-womens/games-of-the-xxist-olympiad-1976.aspx|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20150426091134/http://www.usab.com/history/national-team-womens/games-of-the-xxist-olympiad-1976.aspx|url-status=dead|archive-date=April 26, 2015|title=Games of the XXIst Olympiad – 1976|date=February 20, 2014|work=USA Basketball|access-date=August 7, 2017}}</ref><ref>{{Cite web|url=https://archive.ihf.info/en-us/mediacentre/theroadto/olympicgames2012/historyoftheolympicgames/handballtournamentsatthemontreal1976olympic.aspx|title=Handball Tournaments at the Montreal 1976 Olympic Games|website=International Handball Federation|access-date=August 7, 2017}}</ref> Women also competed for the first time in [[Rowing at the 1976 Summer Olympics|rowing]], participating in six of the eight disciplines.<ref>{{Cite web|url=http://www.rowinghistory-aus.info/olympic-games/1976-Montreal.php|title=1976 Montreal Olympic Games|last=Guerin|first=Andrew|website=Australian Rowing History|access-date=August 9, 2017|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20180830145955/http://www.rowinghistory-aus.info/olympic-games/1976-Montreal.php|archive-date=August 30, 2018|url-status=dead}}</ref> [[Nadia Comăneci]], a Romanian gymnast, made history at the 1976 Montreal Olympics by scoring the first perfect 10.0 in Olympic gymnastics. Her remarkable performance, which included seven perfect scores and three gold medals, elevated the global profile of women's gymnastics. Comăneci's achievements set new standards in the sport and continue to inspire future generations of gymnasts. There were no new events for women at the [[1980 Winter Olympics]] held in Lake Placid. At the [[1980 Summer Olympics]] held in Moscow, [[Field hockey at the 1980 Summer Olympics|women's field hockey]] debuted. The underdog [[Zimbabwe women's national field hockey team at the 1980 Summer Olympics|Zimbabwean team]] pulled off a major upset, winning the gold, the nation's first ever Olympic medal.<ref>{{Cite web|url=http://www.sundaymail.co.zw/1980sofarsogood-so-zimbabwe-so-gold-golden-girl-relives-1980-moscow-olympics/|title=#1980SoFarSoGood: So Zimbabwe, so gold!…Golden Girl relives 1980 Moscow Olympics|last=Bulla|first=Fatima|date=April 12, 2015|website=The Sunday Mail|access-date=August 11, 2017}}</ref> However, these Olympics were marred by the [[1980 Summer Olympics boycott|US-led boycott]] of the games due to the [[Soviet–Afghan War|Soviet invasion of Afghanistan]].<ref>{{Cite web|url=https://2001-2009.state.gov/r/pa/ho/time/qfp/104481.htm|title=The Olympic Boycott, 1980|website=2001-2009.state.gov|access-date=August 11, 2017|date=2008-05-08}}</ref>

=== 1984–1992 ===
[[File:Mary Lou Retton doing a beam routine.jpg|thumb|left|upright|[[Mary Lou Retton]] performing splits on a balance beam, 1985.]]

The [[Cross-country skiing at the 1984 Winter Olympics – Women's 20 km|women's 20 kilometre cross-country skiing event]] was added to the programme for the [[Cross-country skiing at the 1984 Winter Olympics – Women's 20 km|1984 Winter Games]] in Sarajevo. [[Marja-Liisa Kirvesniemi|Marja-Liisa Hämäläinen]] of Finland dominated the [[Cross-country skiing at the 1984 Winter Olympics|cross-country events]], winning gold in all three distances.<ref>{{Cite journal|title=The Official Report of the Organising Committee of the XlVth Winter Olympic Games 1984 at Sarajevo|url=http://library.la84.org/6oic/OfficialReports/1984/or1984w.pdf|journal=Sarajevo 1984|pages=1–198|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20130926190239/http://library.la84.org/6oic/OfficialReports/1984/or1984w.pdf|archive-date=September 26, 2013|url-status=dead}}</ref>

Multiple new events for women were introduced at the [[1984 Summer Olympics]] in Los Angeles. [[Synchronized swimming at the 1984 Summer Olympics|Synchronized swimming]] made its debut, with only women competing in the competition.<ref>{{Cite news|url=https://olympics.time.com/2012/07/16/really-strange-sports-that-are-longer-in-the-olympics/slide/solo-synchronized-swimming/|title=9 Really Strange Sports That Are No Longer in the Olympics|last=Gibson|first=Megan|date=July 6, 2012|magazine=Time|access-date=August 12, 2017|issn=0040-781X}}</ref> The host Americans won gold in both the [[Synchronized swimming at the 1984 Summer Olympics – Women's solo|solo]] and [[Synchronized swimming at the 1984 Summer Olympics – Women's duet|duet]] events.<ref>{{Cite news|url=https://www.washingtonpost.com/wp-srv/sports/olympics/longterm/synchro/synchhis.htm|title=U.S. Synchronized Swimming History|newspaper=The Washington Post|access-date=August 12, 2017}}</ref> Women also made their debut in cycling, competing in the [[Cycling at the 1984 Summer Olympics – Women's individual road race|road race]].<ref name=":4">{{Cite news|url=https://socalbicyclist.com/profiles/events/1984-olympics-women-road-race|title=Reliving The Magic of the First Women's Olympic Cycling Race|last=O'Toole|first=Kelley|date=September 1, 2016|work=BICYCLIST: SoCal and Beyond|access-date=August 12, 2017|archive-date=June 27, 2018|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20180627204001/http://socalbicyclist.com/profiles/events/1984-olympics-women-road-race|url-status=dead}}</ref> This event was also won by an American, [[Connie Carpenter-Phinney|Connie Carpenter]].<ref name=":4" /> [[Gymnastics at the 1984 Summer Olympics – Women's rhythmic individual all-around|Rhythmic gymnastics]] also appeared for the first time with only women competing; the winner was Canadian [[Lori Fung]].<ref>{{Cite news|url=http://olympic.ca/games/1984-los-angeles/|title=Los Angeles 1984|date=August 10, 2011|publisher=Canadian Olympic Committee|access-date=August 12, 2017}}</ref> The [[Athletics at the 1984 Summer Olympics – Women's marathon|women's marathon]] made its first appearance in these Games, with American [[Joan Benoit]] winning gold in 2:24:52, a time many thought was impossible for women just a few years earlier.<ref>{{cite book |author1=David Martin |author2=Roger Glynn |year=2000 |url=https://books.google.com/books?id=Qb125O62NVQC |title=The Olympic Marathon: The History and Drama of Sport's Most Challenging Event |publisher=Human Kinetics |location=Champaign, Illinois|isbn=9780880119696 }}</ref><ref>{{Cite news|url=https://www.runnersworld.com/newswire/the-legacy-of-joan-benoit-samuelsons-olympic-marathon-win|title=The Legacy of Joan Benoit Samuelson's Olympic Marathon Win|last=Robinson|first=Roger|date=August 5, 2014|work=Runner's World|access-date=August 12, 2017}}</ref> These were also the first Games where women competed only against other women in [[Shooting at the 1984 Summer Olympics|shooting]].<ref name=":6">{{Cite web|url=http://www.cnn.com/2013/10/01/world/summer-olympics-competitions-fast-facts/index.html|title=Summer Olympics Competitions Fast Facts|date=July 31, 2017|work=CNN|access-date=August 12, 2017}}</ref> These games were boycotted by the Soviet Union and its satellite states. At the 1984 Los Angeles Olympics, women made up 23% of the participants.

There were no new events at the [[1988 Winter Olympics]] held in Calgary. At the 1988 Summer Olympics in Seoul, [[Table tennis at the 1988 Summer Olympics|table tennis]] appeared for the first time for both men and women.<ref>{{Cite news|url=https://www.sportskeeda.com/table-tennis/olympic-table-tennis-women-gold-medal-winners-so-far-1988-2012|title=Olympic Table Tennis Women: Gold Medal winners so far (1988–2012)|last=Hanagudu|first=Ashwin|date=August 5, 2016|work=Sportskeeda|access-date=August 12, 2017}}</ref> They competed in the [[Table tennis at the 1988 Summer Olympics – Women's singles|singles]] and [[Table tennis at the 1988 Summer Olympics – Women's doubles|doubles]] events. Also, a female specific sailing event debuted at these Games, the [[Sailing at the 1988 Summer Olympics – Women's 470|women's 470]].<ref>{{Cite news|url=http://www.australiansailing.org/about-us/olympic-sailing/|title=Olympic Sailing|work=Australian Sailing Team|access-date=August 12, 2017|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20180721082536/http://www.australiansailing.org/about-us/olympic-sailing/|archive-date=July 21, 2018|url-status=dead}}</ref> For the first time women competed in a track cycling event, [[Cycling at the 1988 Summer Olympics – Women's sprint|the sprint]].<ref name="Track Cycling at the Olympics">{{Cite web|url=http://www.topendsports.com/events/summer/sports/cycling-track.htm|title=Track Cycling at the Olympics|website=topendsports.com|access-date=August 15, 2017}}</ref>

[[File:Dara Torres.jpg|thumb|[[Dara Torres]] celebrated not only for her athletic achievements but also for defying age norms in competitive sports.]]
In 1991, the IOC made it mandatory for all new sports applying for Olympic recognition to have female competitors.<ref>{{Cite book|url=https://books.google.com/books?id=g22hCAAAQBAJ&q=Inclusion+and+Exclusion+in+Competitive+Sport:+Socio-Legal+and+Regulatory+Perspectives|title=Inclusion and Exclusion in Competitive Sport: Socio-Legal and Regulatory Perspectives|last=Patel|first=Seema|date=April 24, 2015|publisher=Routledge|isbn=9781317686330}}</ref> This rule only applied to new sports applying for Olympic recognition, meaning that any sport that was included in the Olympic programme prior to 1991 could continue to exclude female participants at the discretion of the sport's federation.<ref>{{Cite news|url=https://globalnews.ca/news/1142320/womens-ski-jumping-makes-historic-olympic-debut/|title=Women's ski jumping makes historic Olympic debut|last=Loney|first=Heather|date=February 11, 2014|work=Global News|access-date=November 6, 2017}}</ref> At the [[1992 Winter Olympics]] in Albertville, women competed in [[Biathlon at the 1992 Winter Olympics|biathlon]] for the first time.<ref>{{Cite web|url=http://biathloncanada.ca/women-in-biathlon-history/|title=Women in Biathlon (history)|website=Biathlon Canada|date=July 2, 2015|access-date=August 17, 2017|archive-date=December 19, 2018|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20181219142039/http://biathloncanada.ca/women-in-biathlon-history/|url-status=dead}}</ref> The athletes competed in the [[Biathlon at the 1992 Winter Olympics – Women's individual|individual]], [[Biathlon at the 1992 Winter Olympics – Women's sprint|sprint]] and [[Biathlon at the 1992 Winter Olympics – Women's relay|relay]] events. Freestyle skiing also debuted at the 1992 Games, where women competed in the [[Freestyle skiing at the 1992 Winter Olympics – Women's moguls|moguls event]].<ref>{{Cite web|url=http://www.freestylecanada.ski/en/corporate/sport/history/|title=History|website=Freestyle Canada|access-date=August 17, 2017}}</ref> [[Short track speed skating at the 1992 Winter Olympics|Short-track speed skating]] first appeared at these Games.<ref>{{Cite web|url=http://www.speedskating.ca/about/speed-skating-the-sport/short-track-history|title=Short Track History|website=Speed Skating Canada|access-date=August 17, 2017|archive-date=May 29, 2018|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20180529045729/http://www.speedskating.ca/about/speed-skating-the-sport/short-track-history|url-status=dead}}</ref> Women competed in the [[Short track speed skating at the 1992 Winter Olympics – Women's 500 metres|500 metres]] and the [[Short track speed skating at the 1992 Winter Olympics – Women's 3000 metre relay|3000 metre relay]]. At the [[1992 Summer Olympics]] held in Barcelona, [[Badminton at the 1992 Summer Olympics|badminton]] appeared on the programme for the first time.<ref name=":5">{{Cite web|url=http://olympics.bwfbadminton.com/history/#1457668518903-d9caaf49-8e76|title=Badminton – the Olympic Journey|website=Badminton World Federation|access-date=August 18, 2017}}</ref> Women competed in the [[Badminton at the 1992 Summer Olympics – Women's Singles|singles]] and [[Badminton at the 1992 Summer Olympics – Women's Doubles|doubles]] competitions.<ref name=":5" /> Women also competed in the sport of [[Judo at the 1992 Summer Olympics|judo]] for the first time at these Games after women's judo was a [[demonstration sport]] in 1988.<ref>{{Cite news|url=https://www.independent.co.uk/sport/olympics-barcelona-1992-judo-painful-end-for-brave-briggs-1537802.html|title=OLYMPICS / Barcelona 1992: Judo: Painful end for brave Briggs|last=Nicksan|first=Philip|date=August 3, 1992|work=The Independent|access-date=August 18, 2017|archive-date=June 16, 2018|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20180616164958/https://www.independent.co.uk/sport/olympics-barcelona-1992-judo-painful-end-for-brave-briggs-1537802.html|url-status=dead}}</ref> 35 nations still sent all-male delegations to these Games.<ref>{{Cite news|url=https://www.nytimes.com/2008/05/19/opinion/19iht-edahmed.3.13017836.html|title=Bar countries that ban women men and women, and the only time a mixed shooting competition at the Olympics was won by a woman: [[Zhang Shan]].<ref>{{Cite web |title=Barcelona 1992 Shooting Results |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20200418124615/https://www.sports-reference.com/olympics/summer/1992/SHO/mixed-skeet.html |url-status=dead |archive-date=18 April 2020 |title=Shooting at the 1992 Barcelona Summer Games: Mixed Skeet |work=Sports Reference |access-date=26 February 2020}}</ref>

=== 1994–2002 ===
[[File:MiaHamm Stl 1998.jpg|thumb|left|alt=Hamm warming up before a match, 1998|[[Mia Hamm]] warming up before a match, 1998.]]

At the [[1994 Winter Olympics]] in Lillehammer, the [[Freestyle skiing at the 1994 Winter Olympics – Women's aerials|aerials event]] in [[Freestyle skiing at the 1994 Winter Olympics|freestyle skiing]] officially debuted.<ref>{{Cite episode|title=Freestyle skiing goes Olympic|url=http://www.cbc.ca/player/play/1466906059/|access-date=August 21, 2017|series=Front Page Challenge|last=Davis|first=Fred|last2=Berton|first2=Pierre|last3=Fotheringham|first3=Allan|last4=Kennedy|first4=Betty|last5=Webster|first5=Jack|last6=Laroche|first6=Philippe|network=Canadian Broadcasting Corporation|date=January 8, 1994}}</ref> [[Lina Cheryazova]] of Uzbekistan won the gold medal, which is to date her nation's sole medal at an Olympic Winter Games.<ref>{{Cite book|url=https://books.google.com/books?id=fH3hD3MkIMYC|title=Historical Dictionary of the Olympic Movement|last1=Ian|first1=Buchanan|last2=Mallon|first2=Bill|publisher=Scarecrow Press|year=2005|isbn=9780810865242|edition=3rd|pages=306–307|access-date=September 6, 2017}}</ref>

[[File:SwedenUSAwomenshockey2002.jpg|right|thumb|Sweden playing the United States in ice hockey at the 2002 Winter Olympics in Salt Lake City.]]

The promotion of women became a key mission of the [[International Olympic Committee]] (IOC) and was enshrined in the Olympic Charter. By the 1996 Atlanta Olympics, this commitment was evident as the proportion of female participants had increased to 26%.<ref name="Track Cycling at the Olympics"/> The 1996 Olympics in Atlanta represented a watershed moment for [[Football at the 1996 Summer Olympics – Women's tournament|women's football]] and for [[Softball at the 1996 Summer Olympics|softball]], marking its official debut as an Olympic sport.<ref>{{Cite news|url=https://www.fourfourtwo.com/us/features/uswnt-1996-olympic-team-gold-medal-where-are-they-now|title=Where Are They Now? USWNT's 1996 Olympic team|last=Dure|first=Beau|date=July 29, 2016|work=FourFourTwo|access-date=September 6, 2017|archive-date=March 25, 2019|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20190325195845/https://www.fourfourtwo.com/us/features/uswnt-1996-olympic-team-gold-medal-where-are-they-now|url-status=dead}}</ref><ref>{{Cite news|url=https://www.ncaa.com/news/softball/2011-07-19/history-lesson|title=History lesson|last=Hughes|first=Amy|date=July 19, 2011|work=NCAA.com|access-date=September 6, 2017|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20180616103722/https://www.ncaa.com/news/softball/2011-07-19/history-lesson|archive-date=June 16, 2018|url-status=dead}}</ref> The United States women's national soccer team clinched a historic gold medal in a thrilling final against China. This victory was not only the team's first-ever Olympic gold in women's football but also a triumph achieved on home soil in Atlanta.<ref>{{Cite web |title=The Rise of Women's Professional Sports – Equal Playing Fields |url=https://exhibits.library.gsu.edu/equal-playing-fields/womens-professional-sports/ |access-date=2024-06-11 |language=en}}</ref> The success of the U.S. team laid a solid foundation for the growth of women's football on the international stage, inspiring young athletes and paving the way for further advancements in women's sports.

At the [[1998 Winter Olympics]] in Nagano, [[Ice hockey at the 1998 Winter Olympics – Women's tournament|ice hockey]] (with the United States winning gold) and [[Curling at the 1998 Winter Olympics|curling]] (with Canada winning gold) debuted for women with [[Snowboarding at the 1998 Winter Olympics|snowboarding]] also being introduced for both women and men.<ref>{{Cite web|url=https://www.nhl.com/news/impact-of-1998-womens-team-can-still-be-felt-today/c-515423|title=Impact of 1998 women's team can still be felt today|last=McGourty|first=John|date=January 29, 2010|publisher=National Hockey League|access-date=September 6, 2017}}</ref><ref>{{Cite web|url=http://www.worldcurling.org/curling-at-the-olympic-winter-games|title=Curling at the Olympic Games|website=World Curling Federation|access-date=September 6, 2017|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20140211230454/http://www.worldcurling.org/curling-at-the-olympic-winter-games|archive-date=February 11, 2014|url-status=dead}}</ref> Numerous new events made their premieres at the [[2000 Summer Olympics]] in Sydney. [[Weightlifting at the 2000 Summer Olympics|Weightlifting]], [[Modern pentathlon at the 2000 Summer Olympics – Women's|modern pentathlon]], [[Taekwondo at the 2000 Summer Olympics|taekwondo]], [[Triathlon at the 2000 Summer Olympics – Women's|triathlon]] and [[Gymnastics at the 2000 Summer Olympics – Women's trampoline|trampoline]] all debuted in Australia.<ref>{{Cite news|url=https://www.britannica.com/event/Sydney-2000-Olympic-Games|title=Sydney 2000 Olympic Games|date=June 21, 2017|work=Encyclopædia Britannica|access-date=September 10, 2017|publisher=Encyclopædia Britannica, inc.}}</ref> A [[Water polo at the 2000 Summer Olympics – Women's tournament|women's water polo tournament]] was held for the first time. At the [[2002 Winter Olympics]] in Salt Lake City, women's [[Bobsleigh at the 2002 Winter Olympics|bobsleigh]] made its first appearance.<ref>{{Cite news|url=http://news.bbc.co.uk/winterolympics2002/hi/english/bobsleigh/newsid_1830000/1830688.stm|title=US women grab first bobsleigh gold|date=February 20, 2002|publisher=BBC Sport|access-date=September 29, 2017}}</ref> [[Jill Bakken]] and [[Vonetta Flowers]] of the USA won the [[Bobsleigh at the 2002 Winter Olympics – Two-woman|two-woman competition]], the sole bobsleigh event for women at the 2002 Games.<ref>{{Cite news|url=http://www.teamusa.org/USA-Bobsled-Skeleton-Federation/Athletes/Olympic-Teams/2002-Olympic-Team|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20160507075755/http://www.teamusa.org/USA-Bobsled-Skeleton-Federation/Athletes/Olympic-Teams/2002-Olympic-Team|url-status=dead|archive-date=May 7, 2016|title=2002 Olympic Team|publisher=United States Olympic Committee|access-date=September 29, 2017}}</ref>

=== 2004–2012 ===
[[File:BjoergenSprintGold2010.jpg|thumb|[[Marit Bjørgen]] former [[Norway|Norwegian]] [[cross-country skier]] celebrates sprint gold at the [[2010 Winter Olympics|2010 Olympics]].]]

From 2004 to 2012, the Olympic Games witnessed pivotal advancements for women in sports, symbolising a transformative period towards greater gender equity and inclusivity. The [[2004 Summer Olympics|2004 Athens Summer Olympics]] marked historic firsts as women's [[Wrestling at the 2004 Summer Olympics|wrestling]] and sabre fencing were introduced.<ref>{{Cite web|url=http://www.espn.com/olympics/summer04/wrestling/news/story?id=1840536|title=Women's wrestling makes Olympic debut|last=Caple|first=Jim|date=July 15, 2004|work=ESPN|access-date=September 30, 2017}}</ref><ref>{{Cite web|url=http://content.themat.com/section.php?section_id=3&page=showarticle&ArticleID=8545|title=Early 2004 U.S. Olympic Team Trials – Wrestling preview for women's freestyle|last=Weitekamp|first=Jeff|date=November 18, 2003|website=TheMat.com|access-date=September 30, 2017|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20180627173420/http://content.themat.com/section.php?section_id=3&page=showarticle&ArticleID=8545|archive-date=June 27, 2018|url-status=dead}}</ref> These milestones not only expanded the range of sports available to women but also challenged cultural barriers, exemplified by [[Mariel Zagunis]] of the USA winning gold in sabre fencing, showcasing women's athletic prowess on a global stage.<ref>{{Cite web|url=https://www.fencing.net/99/usfa-announces-the-2004-olympic-fencing-team/|title=USFA Announces the 2004 Olympic Fencing Team|last=Harkins|first=Craig|date=April 26, 2004|website=Fencing.net|access-date=September 30, 2017}}</ref><ref>{{Cite web|url=https://sports.yahoo.com/olympics/athens2004/fencing/results?day=17|title=Yahoo! Sports Athens 2004 Summer Olympics Fencing Results|date=August 17, 2004|website=Yahoo Sports|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20121011153958/https://sports.yahoo.com/olympics/athens2004/fencing/results?day=17|archive-date=October 11, 2012|access-date=September 30, 2017}}</ref> In 2004, women from [[Afghanistan at the 2004 Summer Olympics|Afghanistan]] competed at the Olympics for the first time in their history after the nation was banned from Sydney 2000 by the IOC due to the [[Taliban]] government's [[Taliban treatment of women|opposition to women]] in sports.<ref>{{Cite news|url=https://www.theguardian.com/sport/2004/aug/19/athensolympics2004.olympicgames1|title=Forty-two seconds that put Afghan women on the map|last=Donegan|first=Lawrence|date=August 19, 2004|work=The Guardian|access-date=September 30, 2017|issn=0261-3077}}</ref>

In the subsequent [[2008 Summer Olympics]], the inclusion of [[Cycling at the 2008 Summer Olympics – Women's BMX|BMX cycling]], women's [[Athletics at the 2008 Summer Olympics – Women's 3000 metres steeplechase|3000 m steeplechase]], and the [[Swimming at the 2008 Summer Olympics – Women's marathon 10 kilometre|10 kilometre marathon swim]] further diversified the Games, providing new platforms for female athletes to excel.<ref>{{Cite web|url=http://www.usacycling.org/usa-cycling-announces-2008-us-olympic-team.htm|title=USA Cycling announces 2008 U.S. Olympic Team|date=July 1, 2008|website=USA Cycling|access-date=September 30, 2017|archive-date=December 29, 2015|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20151229103851/http://www.usacycling.org/usa-cycling-announces-2008-us-olympic-team.htm|url-status=dead}}</ref><ref name=":6" /><ref>{{Cite web|url=https://www.britishswimming.org/browse-sport/swimming/marathon-and-open-water-swimming/|title=Marathon Swimming|website=British Swimming|access-date=September 30, 2017}}</ref> [[Baseball]] and [[boxing]] remained the only sports not open to women at these Games.

[[File:AshleighMcIvor2010 Contamines2.JPG|upright|thumb|left|[[Ashleigh McIvor]] becoming the first woman to win a gold medal in ski cross, a sport that debuted at those Games.]]
The [[2010 Winter Olympics|2010 Vancouver Winter Olympics]] continued this trend with the debut of [[Freestyle skiing at the 2010 Winter Olympics – Women's ski cross|ski cross]] for both men and women.<ref>{{Cite news|url=http://www.ctvnews.ca/ski-cross-to-debut-at-vancouver-2010-olympics-1.272905|title=Ski cross to debut at Vancouver 2010 Olympics|date=February 3, 2008|work=CTVNews|access-date=October 9, 2017|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20180226211753/https://www.ctvnews.ca/ski-cross-to-debut-at-vancouver-2010-olympics-1.272905|archive-date=2018-02-26|url-status=dead}}</ref> [[Ashleigh McIvor]] of Canada won the inaugural gold for women in the sport.<ref>{{Cite news|url=http://www.cbc.ca/sports/olympics/winter/skiing/ski-cross-olympic-gold-medallist-ashleigh-mcivor-retires-1.1134801|title=Ski cross Olympic gold medallist Ashleigh McIvor retires|last=Shifman|first=Jordan|date=November 16, 2012|work=CBC Sports|access-date=October 9, 2017}}</ref> Controversy was created when women's [[ski jumping]] was excluded from the programme by the IOC due to the low number of athletes and participating nations in the sport.<ref>{{Cite web|url=http://www.iht.com/articles/ap/2006/11/28/sports/ME_SPT_OLY_IOC_Meetings.php|title=IOC approves skicross; rejects women's ski jumping|date=November 28, 2006|website=International Herald Tribune|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20080120010257/http://www.iht.com/articles/ap/2006/11/28/sports/ME_SPT_OLY_IOC_Meetings.php|archive-date=January 20, 2008|access-date=October 9, 2017}}</ref> A group of fifteen competitive female ski jumpers later filed a suit against the [[Vancouver Organizing Committee for the 2010 Olympic and Paralympic Winter Games]] on the grounds that it violated the [[Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms]] since men were competing in the same event.<ref>{{Cite news|url=https://content.time.com/time/specials/packages/article/0,28804,1963484_1963490_1963447,00.html|title=Why Can't Women Ski Jump?|date=February 11, 2010|magazine=Time|access-date=October 9, 2017|issn=0040-781X}}</ref> The suit failed, with the judge ruling that the situation was not governed by the Charter.<ref>{{Cite news|url=http://www.cbc.ca/news/canada/british-columbia/female-ski-jumpers-lose-olympic-battle-1.799850|title=Female ski jumpers lose Olympic battle|date=July 10, 2009|publisher=CBC News|access-date=October 9, 2017}}</ref>

By the [[2012 Summer Olympics|2012 London Summer Olympics]], however, strides towards gender parity were evident as [[women's boxing]] made its Olympic debut.<ref>{{Cite news|url=http://www.gilroydispatch.com/sports/pro/women-s-boxing-makes-olympic-debut-to-sellout-crowd/article_afbd64fc-df78-11e1-a352-0019bb30f31a.html|title=Women's boxing makes Olympic debut to sellout crowd|last=Blount|first=Rachel|date=August 5, 2017|work=Gilroy Dispatch|access-date=November 4, 2017|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20171016183605/http://www.gilroydispatch.com/sports/pro/women-s-boxing-makes-olympic-debut-to-sellout-crowd/article_afbd64fc-df78-11e1-a352-0019bb30f31a.html|archive-date=2017-10-16|url-status=dead}}</ref> This, combined with the decision by the IOC to drop baseball from the programme for 2012, meant that women competed in every sport at a Summer Games for the first time.<ref>{{Cite news|url=https://www.independent.co.uk/sport/olympics/news/london-2012-olympics-the-womens-games-7976835.html |archive-url=https://ghostarchive.org/archive/20220525/https://www.independent.co.uk/sport/olympics/news/london-2012-olympics-the-womens-games-7976835.html |archive-date=May 25, 2022 |url-access=subscription |url-status=live|title=London 2012 Olympics: The women's Games|last=Scott-Elliot|first=Robin|date=July 26, 2012|work=The Independent|access-date=November 4, 2017}}</ref> This landmark event was accompanied by a significant global shift, as all national Olympic committees sent female athletes and countries such as [[Brunei]], [[Saudi Arabia]], and [[Qatar]] included women in their delegations for the first time, reflecting a broader embrace of women's sports participation worldwide.<ref>{{Cite news|url=https://www.bbc.com/news/world-middle-east-18813543|title=Saudi women to compete in Games|date=July 12, 2012|work=BBC News|access-date=November 4, 2017}}</ref><ref>{{Cite news|url=https://www.voanews.com/a/saudi-qatar-brunei-to-send-women-to-olympics/1403891.html|title=Saudi Arabia, Qatar, Brunei to Send Women to Olympics|last=Byrd|first=David|date=July 12, 2012|work=VOA|access-date=November 4, 2017}}</ref> In 2012, for the first time, women athletes outnumbered the men on Team USA. By 2012, women constituted approximately 44% of the total athlete population at the Olympics, highlighting substantial progress in overcoming gender barriers and fostering a more inclusive sporting environment. The London Games of 2012 were particularly notable for being referred to as "'''the Women's Games'''," reflecting the substantial advancements in female athlete participation. These developments underscored the Olympic movement's commitment to gender equality, providing women with enhanced opportunities to showcase their talents and inspiring future generations of female athletes to pursue their Olympic dreams.<ref>{{Cite web |url=https://www.unwomen.org/en/paris-2024-olympics-new-era-for-women-in-sport |title=Paris 2024 Olympics: A new era for women in sport |website=unwomen |access-date=September 4, 2024}}</ref>

=== 2014–2018 ===
[[File:CVN80 First Cut of Steel (36828380355).jpg|thumb|[[Katie Ledecky]] (middle) and fellow Olympic champion [[Simone Biles]] (left) sign steel plate of {{USS|Enterprise|CVN-80|6}} (2017).]]

The [[2014 Winter Olympics]] in Sochi saw a groundbreaking moment with [[Carina Vogt]] winning the first-ever gold medal in [[Ski jumping at the 2014 Winter Olympics – Women's normal hill individual|women's ski jumping]], marking the event's long-awaited debut.<ref>{{Cite news|url=https://www.telegraph.co.uk/sport/othersports/winter-olympics/10632212/Sochi-Winter-Olympics-2014-Carina-Vogt-wins-womens-ski-jumping-gold.html|title=Sochi Winter Olympics 2014: Carina Vogt wins women's ski jumping gold|last=Hart|first=Simon|date=February 11, 2017|work=The Telegraph|access-date=November 6, 2017|issn=0307-1235}}</ref><ref>{{Cite news|url=https://www.bbc.com/sport/winter-olympics/26057176|title=Sochi 2014: Carina Vogt wins women's ski jumping gold|last=Hodgetts|first=Rob|date=February 11, 2014|publisher=BBC Sport|access-date=November 6, 2017}}</ref> This achievement was a pivotal step forward for gender equality in winter sports.

The [[2016 Summer Olympics]] followed suit with another historic first as the [[Australia women's national rugby sevens team|Australian women's rugby sevens team]] triumphed in the first Olympic [[Rugby sevens at the 2016 Summer Olympics – Women's tournament|women's rugby tournament]], showcasing the sport's inaugural appearance and emphasising its growing global appeal.<ref>{{Cite news|url=http://bleacherreport.com/articles/1294490-2016-olympics-golf-rugby-and-kitesurfing-events-debuting-in-rio|title=2016 Olympics: New Events Debuting in Rio|last=Shiekman|first=Mike|date=August 12, 2012|work=Bleacher Report|access-date=November 7, 2017}}</ref><ref>{{Cite news|url=http://www.abc.net.au/news/2017-07-27/australian-olympic-rugby-sevens-gold-leads-to-new-national-comp/8748886|title=Aussie Olympic gold leads to national women's sevens tournament|last=Galloway|first=Patrick|date=July 27, 2017|work=ABC News|access-date=November 7, 2017}}</ref> Meanwhile, Rio 2016 also welcomed the return of [[Golf at the 2016 Summer Olympics – Women's individual|golf]] to the Olympic programme after an absence of over a century.<ref>{{Cite news|url=https://www.forbes.com/sites/erikmatuszewski/2016/08/07/ten-things-to-know-as-golf-returns-to-olympics-after-112-year-absence/|title=Ten Things To Know As Golf Returns To Olympics After 112-Year Absence|last=Matuszewski|first=Erik|date=August 7, 2016|work=Forbes|access-date=November 7, 2017}}</ref> [[Inbee Park]] of South Korea seized the opportunity, clinching the gold medal and rekindling interest in golf as an Olympic sport.<ref>{{Cite news|url=http://www.golfchannel.com/article/randall-mell/parks-gold-medal-performance-captivates-nation/|title=Inbee Park's Gold-Medal Performance Captivates a Nation|last=Mell|first=Randall|date=August 21, 2016|work=Golf Channel|access-date=November 7, 2017}}</ref> Women competed in the same number of weight categories as men for the first time in freestyle wrestling.<ref>{{Cite news|url=https://www.washingtonpost.com/sports/olympics/wrestling-ioc-make-right-moves-in-getting-sport-back-on-2020-olympics-program/2013/09/08/a2ed9cc4-18a2-11e3-8685-5021e0c41964_story.html|title=Wrestling, IOC make right moves in getting sport back on 2020 Olympics program|date=September 8, 2013|access-date=August 10, 2024}}</ref> The Games also set a remarkable record for Team USA's female contingent, with 291 women competing across various sports and collectively bringing home an unprecedented 65 medals. Among the standout athletes were [[Katie Ledecky]] and [[Simone Biles]], who each secured five medals, underscoring their dominance in swimming and gymnastics respectively.

At the [[2018 Winter Olympics]] in PyeongChang, the Olympics continued to evolve with the introduction of new events which included big air snowboarding, mass start speed skating, [[mixed doubles curling]] and mixed team alpine skiing. [[Jamie Anderson (snowboarder)|Jamie Anderson]] of the USA emerged as a standout, notching medals in both big air and slopestyle snowboarding, cementing her status as one of the most decorated female snowboarders in Olympic history.<ref>{{Cite magazine|url=https://www.si.com/more-sports/2015/06/08/2018-winter-olympics-new-sports-added|title=Snowboarding, curling mixed doubles among new sports added to 2018 Winter Olympics|date=June 8, 2015|magazine=Sports Illustrated|access-date=November 7, 2017}}</ref><ref>{{Cite news|url=https://www.bbc.com/sport/winter-sports/33056128|title=Winter Olympics: Big air, mixed curling among new 2018 events|date=June 8, 2015|publisher=BBC Sport|access-date=November 7, 2017}}</ref>

=== 2020–present===
[[File:Emma_McKeon_-_2021_World_Cup_-_01.jpg|thumb|left|[[Emma McKeon]] With four gold and three bronze medals she was the most decorated athlete across all sports at the 2020 Summer Olympics, and tied for the most medals won by a woman in a single Olympic Games.]]

In 2020, an IOC policy change permitted one male and one female athlete to jointly carry their nation's flag during the opening ceremony of the [[2020 Summer Olympics|2020 Tokyo Summer Olympics]], resulting in 194 female athletes selected as flag bearers.<ref>{{Cite journal |last=Malanski |first=Daniel |date=2024-04-08 |title=Placard Carriers and Flag Bearers: Gender Representation in the Summer Olympics’ Parade of Nations (1908–2021) |url=https://doi.org/10.1080/09523367.2023.2269099 |journal=The International Journal of the History of Sport |volume=41 |issue=5 |pages=460–489 |doi=10.1080/09523367.2023.2269099 |issn=0952-3367 |doi-access=free}}</ref> The 2020 Olympics introduced four new sports, all with women's events ([[Karate at the 2020 Summer Olympics|karate]], [[Sport climbing at the 2020 Summer Olympics – Women's combined|sport climbing]], [[Surfing at the 2020 Summer Olympics|surfing]], and [[Skateboarding at the 2020 Summer Olympics|skateboarding]]), as well as the one-time reintroduction of [[Softball at the 2020 Summer Olympics|softball]]<ref>{{Cite news |date=August 3, 2016 |title=You're in! Baseball/softball, 4 other sports make Tokyo cut |url=https://www.usatoday.com/story/sports/olympics/2016/08/03/ioc-approves-addition-of-5-sports-for-2020-tokyo-olympics/88035700/ |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20231103151040/https://www.usatoday.com/story/sports/olympics/2016/08/03/ioc-approves-addition-of-5-sports-for-2020-tokyo-olympics/88035700/ |archive-date=November 3, 2023 |access-date=November 7, 2017 |work=USA Today}}</ref> and women's events in the new disciplines of [[3x3 basketball]] and [[freestyle BMX]]. It also introduced nine new mixed-gender events in several sports (archery, judo, three events in shooting, table tennis, triathlon, a 4 × 400 m relay in athletics and a [[Swimming at the 2020 Summer Olympics – Mixed 4 × 100 metre medley relay|4 × 100 medley relay in swimming]]).<ref>{{cite web |title=What is the new swimming mixed medley relay event? |url=https://olympics.com/tokyo-2020/en/news/what-is-the-new-swimming-mixed-medley-relay-event |url-status=dead |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210803001112/https://olympics.com/tokyo-2020/en/news/what-is-the-new-swimming-mixed-medley-relay-event |archive-date=August 3, 2021 |access-date=2 August 2021 |website=olympics.com}}</ref> Several existing sports made attempts to ensure gender parity. A men's 800 metre freestyle event and a women's 1500 metre freestyle event were added in [[Swimming at the 2020 Summer Olympics|swimming]] so that the men's and women's programmes were equal. There were multiple events added in [[Canoeing at the 2020 Summer Olympics|canoeing]] in the [[canoe sprint]] and [[canoe slalom]] disciplines which marked the first time women could compete in canoes at the Olympics,<ref>{{Cite web|url=http://www.womencanintl.com/news/icf-approves-new-race-program-for--olympics-includes--womens-canoe-events/|title=ICF Approves New Race Program for 2020 Olympics – Includes 3 Women's Canoe Events|date=January 14, 2016|website=WomenCAN International|access-date=November 6, 2017|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20190326171018/https://www.womencanintl.com/news/icf-approves-new-race-program-for--olympics-includes--womens-canoe-events/|archive-date=March 26, 2019|url-status=dead}}</ref> something they hadn't bee allowed to do until Tokyo 2020.<ref>{{Cite web|url=http://canoekayak.ca/icf-approves-new-race-programs-for-the-2020-olympic-games-2/|title=ICF approves new race programs for the 2020 Olympic Games|last=Miller|first=Ian|date=January 15, 2016|website=canoekayak.ca|access-date=November 6, 2017|archive-date=September 28, 2017|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20170928105214/http://canoekayak.ca/icf-approves-new-race-programs-for-the-2020-olympic-games-2/|url-status=dead}}</ref><ref>{{Cite web |date=2017-06-09 |title=IOC announcement confirms changes to canoeing program for Tokyo |url=https://www.canoeicf.com/news/ioc-announcement-confirms-changes-canoeing-program-tokyo |access-date=2025-09-24 |website=ICF - Planet Canoe |language=en}}</ref> In canoe sprint, the men's C-1 200 metres and men's K-2 200 metres were replaced with women's C-1 200 metres and women's C-2 500 metres. In canoe slalom, the men's C-2 was removed and the women's C-1 was added. In addition, the sprint men's K-4 1000 metres was replaced with a shorter race, the men's K-4 500 metres to match the women's.<ref name=ProgrammeChange>{{cite news |url=https://www.canoeicf.com/news/ioc-announcement-confirms-changes-canoeing-program-tokyo |title=The IOC's final approval of the event programme for the 2020 Olympics confirms changes to the canoe sprint and slalom rundown for Tokyo. |publisher=International Canoe Federation |date=9 June 2017 |access-date=24 June 2021}}</ref> The men-only [[Shooting at the 2020 Summer Olympics|shooting]] events of the 50 metre pistol and the 50 metre rifle prone were removed as well as the men's double trap (12 years after the women's was removed) and men and women received an equal number of shots.<ref name=ISSF-changes>{{cite news|title=ISSF Welcomes IOC Decisions Regarding Tokyo 2020 Sports Program|url=https://www.issf-sports.org/news.ashx?newsid=2822|publisher=[[International Shooting Sport Federation]]|date=9 June 2017|access-date=25 March 2018}}</ref> Two categories were added to women's [[Boxing at the 2020 Summer Olympics|boxing]] while two were dropped from the men's resulting in five events for women and eight events for men. Women's boxing competitions also took place over three three-minute rounds to match the men's instead of the four rounds of two minutes each which had taken place since women's boxing was first introduced to the Olympics in 2012.<ref>{{Cite news|url=http://www.espn.com/boxing/story/_/id/19150981/nicola-adams-fight-3-minute-rounds-next-bout|title=Adams to fight same 3-minute rounds as men|last=Rafael|first=Dan|date=April 14, 2017|work=ESPN|access-date=November 6, 2017}}</ref> In [[Rowing at the 2020 Summer Olympics|rowing]], the men's lightweight coxless four was removed and the women's coxless four was added for the first time since 1992 to equalise the men's and women's programmes.<ref>{{Cite web |url=https://www.unwomen.org/en/paris-2024-olympics-new-era-for-women-in-sport |title=Paris 2024 Olympics: A new era for women in sport |website=unwomen |access-date=September 4, 2024}}</ref> A women's team foil event and a men's team sabre event were added in [[Fencing at the 2020 Summer Olympics|fencing]]. The [[Madison (cycling)|Madison]] returned to cycling, with a women's event making its debut. The men's singles [[Tennis at the 2020 Summer Olympics|tennis]] final was reduced to a three-set match from five sets which brought it in line with the women's.<ref name="bbc_tennis">{{cite news |date=3 April 2019 |title=Olympics: Men's final reduced to three sets for Tokyo 2020 |url=https://www.bbc.com/sport/tennis/47807003 |access-date=4 August 2021 |agency=British Broadcasting Corporation}}</ref> New Zealand's [[Laurel Hubbard]] became the first openly [[Transgender people in sports|transgender woman to compete at the Olympics]], participating in the [[Weightlifting at the 2020 Summer Olympics – Women's +87 kg|women's +87 kg weightlifting]] event.<ref>{{Cite web |last=Ingle |first=Sean |date=2021-06-20 |title=Weightlifter Laurel Hubbard will be first trans athlete to compete at Olympics |url=http://www.theguardian.com/sport/2021/jun/21/olympics-tokyo-laurel-hubbard-trans-weightlifter-new-zealand |access-date=2021-07-06 |website=The Guardian |language=en}}</ref>

[[File:2020-01-20 Freestyle skiing at the 2020 Winter Youth Olympics – Women's Halfpipe – Qualification – 2nd run (Martin Rulsch) 027.jpg|thumb|[[Eileen Gu]] won three gold medals in freestyle skiing and snowboarding events.]]

The [[International Ski Federation]] stated in 2017 that it aimed to include women's Nordic combined in the Olympic program for the first time at the [[2022 Winter Olympics]] in Beijing.<ref>{{Cite news|url=https://www.insidethegames.biz/articles/1042254/fis-target-nordic-combined-womens-competition-at-beijing-2022|title=FIS target Nordic Combined women's competition at Beijing 2022|last=Pavitt|first=Michael|date=October 3, 2016|work=Inside the Games|access-date=November 7, 2017}}</ref> However, [[Nordic combined at the 2022 Winter Olympics]] ended up having three men-only events, just as in 2018. A new monobob event was introduced in bobsleigh, only open to women. Women accounted for 44.7% of the athletes competing in Beijing and participated in 53.2% of the total combined events.<ref>{{Cite report |url=https://www.womenssportsfoundation.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/12/2022-Olympic-and-Paralympic-Report.pdf |title=Women in the 2022 Olympic and Paralympic Games: An Analysis of Participation, Leadership, and Media Coverage |date=December 2022 |publisher=Women's Sports Foundation}}</ref> Athletes like [[Eileen Gu]] in freestyle skiing/snowboarding and [[Anna Shcherbakova]] in figure skating stood out with their gold medal performances.

The 2024 Games introduced men's participation in [[Artistic swimming at the 2024 Summer Olympics|artistic swimming]] (with a maximum of two male members per eight-person team, although none managed to compete), a new women's weight class in boxing and the removal of one weight class from men's boxing, the replacement of the men's C-2 1000 metre and K-2 1000 metre events in canoeing with C-2 500 metre and K-2 500 metre events for parity with the women's, the removal of the men-only Finn event in [[Sailing at the 2024 Summer Olympics|sailing]], the replacement of the men's and women's 470 sailing events with a mixed-gender 470 event, and the addition of a marathon race walk mixed relay in athletics to replace the men-only [[50 kilometres race walk]] event due to its lack of female participation. Several sports also finally achieved gender parity such as [[Cycling at the 2024 Summer Olympics|cycling]] which instituted several significant changes in the road and track cycling, with some men's quota places transferred to the women's side (each country had previously been limited to sending five men and four women to the Summer Games in road cycling)<ref name="GuardLon"/> and with the number of riders in women's team sprint increasing from two to three.

As of 2024, women constituted 41% of the members of the International Olympic Committee (IOC).<ref>{{Cite web |date=7 March 2024 |title=#GenderEqualOlympics: advancing gender equality beyond the field of play |url=https://www.olympics.com/ioc/news/genderequalolympics-advancing-gender-equality-beyond-the-field-of-play |access-date=23 September 2025 |website=International Olympic Committee}}</ref> The 2024 Paris Olympics were notable for being "the first Olympic Games in history with full gender parity on the field of play".<ref>{{Cite web |date=28 July 2024 |title=#GenderEqualOlympics: Paris 2024 making history on the field of play |url=https://olympics.com/ioc/news/genderequalolympics-paris-2024-making-history-on-the-field-of-play |access-date=September 9, 2024 |website=International Olympic Committee}}</ref><ref>{{Cite web |url=https://www.unwomen.org/en/paris-2024-olympics-new-era-for-women-in-sport |title=Paris 2024 Olympics: A new era for women in sport |website=UN Women |access-date=September 4, 2024}}</ref> Of the 11,215 athletes registered, 5,712 were men and 5,503 were women.<ref>{{Cite web |date=2024-07-18 |title=Paris Olympic competition nears total gender parity. Take a look at the athlete gender breakdown |url=https://apnews.com/article/2024-olympic-games-gender-parity-c194ca5934911efbce801363f28e8c04 |access-date=2024-09-08 |website=AP News |language=en}}</ref> Multiple [[Non-binary gender|non-binary]] and trans-male athletes, such as [[Hergie Bacyadan]], competed in women's events.<ref>{{Cite web |title=All of the LGBTQ Olympians at the 2024 Paris Summer Games |url=https://www.outsports.com/olympics/team-lgbtq/ |access-date=2025-09-24 |website=OutSports |language=en}}</ref> Boxer [[Cindy Ngamba]] won the first ever medal for the [[Refugee Olympic Team]]. The last medal ceremony of the Games was for the women's marathon instead of the men's for the first time, with the last medal of the Games awarded to [[Sifan Hassan]] who represented the Netherlands. The 2024 Olympics showcased 28 gender-balanced sports out of 32, with 157 medal events designated for men, 152 medal events designated for women and 20 mixed-gender events.

[[File:Valarie_Allman_5637_(51917818307).jpg|thumb|left|American track and field athlete [[Valarie Allman]] specializing in the discus throw.]]

At the 2024 Summer Olympics in Paris, Team USA's roster included a greater number of women than men for the fourth consecutive Games. The U.S. team comprised 314 women and 279 men. When considering both mixed and team events, American women contributed to 57% of the 126 medals won by Team USA. In the gold medal tally, American women made a significant impact, securing 26 of the country's 40 gold medals. This performance tied the U.S. with China for the highest number of golds. Notably, swimmer [[Katie Ledecky]] distinguished herself by winning two gold medals, one silver, and one bronze in Paris, thereby becoming the most decorated American woman in Olympic history with 14 career medals. Ledecky's achievements included setting new records, such as in the {{nowrap|4 × 100 m}} medley relay with teammates [[Regan Smith (swimmer)|Regan Smith]], [[Lilly King]], [[Gretchen Walsh]], and [[Torri Huske]]. Smith also set an American record in the 200m butterfly, earning a silver medal. [[Caroline Marks]] secured her second consecutive Olympic gold medal in women's surfing at the 2024 Olympics, defeating [[Tatiana Weston-Webb]] from Brazil. Gymnast Simone Biles further solidified her reputation as one of the greatest gymnasts of all time by winning three gold medals and one silver, totaling 11 career Olympic medals and making her the most decorated American gymnast in Olympic history. Biles, along with [[Suni Lee]], contributed to USA Gymnastics' historic achievement of having two women's all-around gold medalists in a single Olympics. In athletics, [[Sydney McLaughlin-Levrone]] excelled by becoming the first two-time gold medalist in the 400m hurdles, setting a new world record and securing another gold in the {{nowrap|4 × 400 m}} relay. Additionally, [[Valarie Allman|Valerie Allman]] defended her Olympic title in the discus throw, becoming the first American woman to win two gold medals in an Olympic field event.<ref>{{Cite web |url=https://www.nbcchicago.com/paris-2024-summer-olympics/team-usa-women-history-paris-olympics/3521270/ |title=All the ways American women made history at the Paris Olympics |website=nbcchicago |date=August 13, 2024 |access-date=September 6, 2024}}</ref>

The [[2026 Winter Olympics|2026 Milano Cortina Winter Olympics]] featured the return of the men's doubles in [[Luge at the Winter Olympics|luge]] and the introduction of the women's doubles, as the doubles event had been an open gender event since 1994 but only men had competed.<ref>{{cite web |url=http://www.topendsports.com/events/winter/women.htm |publisher=topend sports |title=Women & the Winter Olympics |access-date=February 16, 2018}}</ref> A women's large hill individual event in [[Ski jumping at the Winter Olympics|ski jumping]] was introduced, while the [[Alpine skiing at the Winter Olympics|alpine skiing]] mixed team parallel event was dropped and a mixed relay team event in [[Skeleton at the Winter Olympics|skeleton]] was introduced. For the first time, women raced the same distances as men in all [[Cross-country skiing at the Winter Olympics|cross-country skiing]] events.<ref>{{Cite news |last=Berkeley |first=Geoff |date=20 June 2023 |title=Alpine skiing combined to become team event for Milan Cortina 2026 |url=https://www.insidethegames.biz/articles/1138130/alpine-combined-team-event-milan-2026 |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20240720140501/https://www.insidethegames.biz/articles/1138130/alpine-combined-team-event-milan-2026 |archive-date=20 July 2024 |access-date=31 January 2025 |work=Inside the Games}}</ref> The Games had the highest percentage of women's participation in Winter Olympic history, at 47%.<ref>{{cite web |title=Milano Cortina 2026 set to become the most gender-balanced Olympic Winter Games in history |url=https://olympics.com/ioc/news/milano-cortina-2026-set-to-become-the-most-gender-balanced-olympic-winter-games-in-history |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20220628074149/https://olympics.com/ioc/news/milano-cortina-2026-set-to-become-the-most-gender-balanced-olympic-winter-games-in-history |archive-date=28 June 2022 |access-date=24 June 2022 |website=olympics.com |publisher=International Olympic Committee}}</ref>

== Current gender differences ==

===Alpine skiing===
The men and women ski different distances and sometimes at different venues, for example in the 2026 Winter Olympics for the downhill events the men's course ([[Stelvio (ski course)|Stelvio]]) was 3,442 metres in length, with a vertical drop of 1,023 metres from a starting elevation of 2,268 metres above sea level. The women's course ([[Olimpia delle Tofane]]) was 2,572 metres in length, with a vertical drop of 760 metres from a starting elevation of 2,320 metres above sea level.

===Artistic gymnastics===
Men have six apparatus and women only have four, with both sharing floor and vault but men having pommel horse, parallel bars, rings and horizontal bar and women having balance beam and uneven bars. Women's floor routines are up to 90 seconds long whilst men's floor routines are up to 70 seconds long and don't feature music. Women are scored using more artistic components than men. In the vault, the vaulting table is set higher for men at 1.35 metres and lower for women at 1.25 metres.

===Artistic swimming===
The duet event is women-only. The 2024 Games introduced men's participation in the artistic swimming team event with a maximum of two male members per eight-person team, although no men have competed yet.<ref>{{Cite web |date=2022-12-22 |title=For the first time, men eligible to compete in Artistic Swimming at the Olympic Games in Paris 2024 |url=https://www.worldaquatics.com/news/2990453/for-the-first-time-men-eligible-to-compete-in-artistic-swimming-at-the-olympic-games-in-paris-2024 |access-date=2024-08-16 |website=World Aquatics |language=en}}</ref>

===Athletics===
[[File:08-02-1947 02334B Fanny Blankers-Koen (5580078187).jpg|thumb|left|[[Fanny Blankers-Koen]] made history at the 1948 London Olympics by winning four gold medals in track and field (100m, 200m, 80m hurdles, and {{nowrap|4 × 100 m}} relay).]]

In [[combined events at the Olympics]], women compete in the seven-event [[heptathlon]] but men compete in three more events in the [[decathlon]].<ref name="GuardLon"/> A [[women's pentathlon]] was held from 1964 to 1980, before being expanded to the heptathlon.

[[File:Florence_Griffith_Joyner2.jpg|thumb|[[Florence Griffith Joyner]] known as "Flo-Jo," athlete who set world records in the 100m and 200m sprints at the 1988 Seoul Olympics.]]

In [[sprint hurdles at the Olympics]], men compete in the 110&nbsp;metres hurdles, while women cover 100&nbsp;metres.<ref name="GuardLon"/> Women ran 80&nbsp;metres up to the 1968 Olympics; this was extended to 100&nbsp;metres in 1961, albeit on a trial basis, the new distance of 100&nbsp;metres became official in 1969. Both men and women clear a total of ten hurdles during the races and both genders take three steps between the hurdles at elite level.<ref>{{cite web | url=http://www.ustfccca.org/assets/symposiums/2014/Sprints-Mangiacotti-2014.pdf | title=Rhythmic Hurdling: The Search for the Holy Grail | archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20150519081231/http://www.ustfccca.org/assets/symposiums/2014/Sprints-Mangiacotti-2014.pdf |archive-date=May 19, 2015 |publisher=USTFCCCA}}</ref>

Historically, women competed over [[3000 metres]] until this was matched to the men's [[5000 metres]] event in 1996. Similarly, women competed in a [[10 kilometres race walk]] in 1992 and 1996 before this was changed to the standard men's distance of 20&nbsp;km. The expansion of the women's athletics programme to match the men's was a slow one. Triple jump was added in 1996, hammer throw and pole vault in 2000, and steeplechase in 2008. The last difference remaining (excluding the heptathlon/decathlon, hurdle and steeplechase barrier heights, the distance between the take off line and landing area in triple jump and throwing implement differences in discus throw, hammer throw, javelin throw and shot put which still persist) was the men-only [[50 kilometres race walk]] event, which was removed from the Olympics in 2024 due to lack of female participation and replaced with the [[marathon race walk mixed relay]], which was itself removed after 2024.<ref>{{cite web | url=https://www.iaaf.org/disciplines/race-walks/50-kilometres-race-walk | title=50 Kilometres Race Walk | publisher=IAAF}}</ref><ref>{{cite web|last=Kelly | first=Daniel |date=April 17, 2017 | url=https://www.offtheball.com/golf/50-km-race-may-be-removed-from-the-olympic-program-250022 | title=50 km race may be removed from the Olympic program | publisher=Off The Ball}}</ref><ref>{{cite web | last=Philips | first=Mitch | date=April 13, 2017 | url=https://www.reuters.com/article/us-athletics-iaaf-walk-idUSKBN17F278  |title=Athletics – 50km walk to stay in Olympics | publisher=Reuters | access-date=March 22, 2018}}</ref><ref>{{cite web|url=https://www.insidethegames.biz/articles/1135696/olympic-race-walking-event-paris-2024|title=New Olympic marathon race walk mixed relay for Paris 2024 revealed|website=[[insidethegames.biz]] |date=April 8, 2023 }}</ref>

===Baseball===
The baseball tournament is only open to men.

=== Basketball ===
Women use smaller basketballs than men (size 6 rather than size 7).

===Beach volleyball===
The men's net is set to a height of 2.43 metres. The women's net is set lower, at 2.24 metres.

===Biathlon===
Women compete at shorter distances than men in all events apart from in the mixed relay. The men's individual event is 20 kilometres whereas the women's is 15 kilometres, the men's sprint is 10 kilometres and the women's is 7.5 kilometres, the pursuit is 12.5 kilometres for men and 10 kilometres for women, the mass start is 15 kilometres for men and 12.5 kilometres for women, and the men's relay is 4 x 7.5 kilometres whereas the women's relay is 4 x 6 kilometres.

===Bobsleigh===
The monobob event is only open to women and the four-man bobsleigh event has no equivalent for women. The two-woman bobsleigh event is held with lighter, slightly smaller sleds than the two-men bobsleigh event with a lower minimum weight requirement. The two-man event has a maximum combined weight (crew, sled and equipment) of 390 kilograms and the two-woman event has a maximum combined weight of 340 kilograms.

===Boxing===
When women's boxing was first introduced to the Olympics in 2012, women competed in three weight categories against 10 for men.<ref name="GuardLon">{{cite news|url=https://www.theguardian.com/society/2012/aug/10/london-2012-women-men-equality|title=London 2012: not all Olympians compete on an equal footing|last=Davies|first=Lizzy|date=August 10, 2012|work=The Guardian|access-date=August 3, 2017}}</ref> Two male categories were removed in 2020 while two female categories were added. In 2024, another weight class was added to women's boxing and one was removed from the men's. Currently men compete in seven events and women compete in six with another women's event planned to be added in 2028 to achieve gender parity in the sport.<ref>{{cite web|title=LA28 Medal Event program updates|url=https://la28.org/en/newsroom/los-angeles-2028-medal-event-program-and-athlete-quota.html|website=la28.org|publisher=Los Angeles Organizing Committee for the 2028 Olympic and Paralympic Games|access-date=April 9, 2025|date=|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20250409173954/https://la28.org/en/newsroom/los-angeles-2028-medal-event-program-and-athlete-quota.html|archive-date=April 9, 2025|url-status=live}}</ref> The weight classes in 2028 will be 55 kg, 60 kg, 65 kg, 70 kg, 80 kg, 90 kg and +90 kg for the men and 51 kg, 54 kg, 57 kg, 60 kg, 65 kg, 70 kg and 75 kg for the women.<ref>{{cite web|title=IOC confirms gender parity in boxing at LA28 with seven weight classes for both men and women|url=https://worldboxing.org/ioc-confirms-gender-parity-in-boxing-at-la28-with-seven-weight-classes-for-both-men-and-women/|website=worldboxing.org|publisher=World Boxing|access-date=February 15, 2026}}</ref> Women are required to wear headguards whilst men were last required to do so in 2016.<ref>{{cite news|url=https://www.espn.com/olympics/story/_/id/9087686/olympic-boxing-drops-head-guards-changes-scoring-system|title=Olympic boxing drops head guards|publisher=[[ESPN]]|date=23 March 2013|access-date=30 January 2015}}</ref> Women wear 10 ounce boxing gloves for all weight classes and men wear twelve ounce gloves from 70 kg upwards.

===Canoe sprint===
[[File:Birgit_Fischer_(2010).jpg|thumb|[[Birgit Fischer]] competed in various canoeing events and is one of the most successful Olympians in terms of gold medals won.]]
Only women can compete in the C-1 200 metres event and K-1 500 metres event and only men can compete in the C-1 1000 metres event and the K-1 1000 metres event.

===Figure skating===
The multiplier applied to program component scores is 1.33 for the women's short program, 1.67 for the men's short program, 2.67 for the women's free skate and 3.33 for the men's free skate. Quadruple jumps are not allowed in the women's short program and they have different spin requirements.

=== Football ===
In Olympic football, there is no age restriction for women, whereas the men's teams field under-23 teams with a maximum of three over-aged players.<ref>{{Cite news|url=http://www.foxsports.com/soccer/story/what-are-the-rules-for-olympics-men-s-soccer-080416|title=What are the rules for Olympics men's soccer?|date=August 4, 2016|work=Fox Sports|access-date=August 1, 2017|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20160806003206/http://www.foxsports.com/soccer/story/what-are-the-rules-for-olympics-men-s-soccer-080416|archive-date=2016-08-06|url-status=dead}}</ref> The men's tournament contains sixteen teams whereas the women's tournament contains twelve teams. This will be reversed in 2028 due to the growing popularity of [[Women's association football|women's football]].<ref>{{cite web|title=LA28 Medal Event program updates|url=https://la28.org/en/newsroom/los-angeles-2028-medal-event-program-and-athlete-quota.html|website=la28.org|publisher=Los Angeles Organizing Committee for the 2028 Olympic and Paralympic Games|access-date=April 9, 2025|date=|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20250409173954/https://la28.org/en/newsroom/los-angeles-2028-medal-event-program-and-athlete-quota.html|archive-date=April 9, 2025|url-status=live}}</ref>

=== Freestyle wrestling ===
Women are not allowed to use neck holds (double nelsons). The weight classes are 57 kg, 65 kg, 74 kg, 86 kg, 97 kg and 125 kg for the men and 50 kg, 53 kg, 57 kg, 62 kg, 68 kg and 76 kg for the women.

=== Golf ===
Women play shorter total distances than men as they play from forward tees, reducing the overall course length by roughly 800 yards compared to the men's [[tee|tees]].

=== Greco-Roman wrestling ===
Women are excluded from Greco-Roman wrestling, with men competing in six events.

=== Handball ===
Women use smaller balls than men (size 2 rather than size 3).

===Ice hockey===
The men's teams can have up to 25 players whereas the women's can have 23. Women compete in a 10-team tournament, whereas men compete in a 12-team tournament. Body checking is not allowed in women's ice hockey.

===Indoor volleyball===
The men's net is set to a height of 2.43 metres. The women's net is set lower, at 2.24 metres.

=== Judo ===
The weight classes are 60 kg, 66 kg, 73 kg, 81 kg, 90 kg, 100 kg and +100 kg for the men and 48 kg, 52 kg, 57 kg, 63 kg, 70 kg, 78 kg and +78 kg for the women.

===Luge===
In singles, men start at a higher point on the track, resulting in a longer course and higher top speeds. Women’s singles start from a lower point. Men are required to have a minimum weight of 90 kilograms while women must weigh at least 75 kilograms. If below this, they are allowed to carry extra weight.

===Mountain biking===
Women race 1-2 fewer laps than men.

===Nordic combined===
Only men are allowed to compete in the three nordic combined events.

===Rhythmic gymnastics===
Only women are allowed to compete in the two rhythmic gymnastics events.

===Road cycling===
Since 1984, when women's cycling events were introduced, the women's road race has been approximately 140 kilometres to the men's approximately 250 kilometres, with the men's course also having a higher elevation. The time trials were equalised in 2024.

===Sailing===
For the men's single-handed dinghy event, the equipment used is the [[ILCA 7]] while the women use the [[ILCA 6]]. For the men's skiff event, the equipment used is the [[49er (dinghy)|49er]] while the women use the [[49er (dinghy)|49erFX]].

===Shooting===
Women are excluded from the 25 metre rapid fire pistol event. Men are excluded from the 25 metre pistol event. In the 50 metre rifle three positions event, men's rifles can weigh up to 8 kilograms, while women's rifles are capped at 6.5 kilograms.

===Short-track speed skating===
Women compete in the 3000 metre relay whereas men compete in the 5000 metre relay.

===Skeleton===
Men can use sleds up to 45 kilograms with a maximum combined athlete and sled weight of 120 kilograms and women can use sleds up to 38 kilograms with a maximum combined athlete and sled weight of 102 kilograms.

===Ski jumping===
There are three men-only events but only two women-only events as there is no women's large hill super team event equivalent to its male counterpart.

===Softball===
The softball tournament is only open to women.

===Speed skating===
Women compete in the 3000 metres whereas men compete in the 10,000 metres.

===Sport climbing===
Men and women climb different boulder and lead routes.

=== Taekwondo ===
The weight classes are 58 kg, 68 kg, 80 kg and +80 kg for the men and 49 kg, 57 kg, 67 kg and +67 kg for the women.

===Track cycling===
Women race fewer laps than men in two events. In the [[Madison (cycling)|Madison]], women race 120 laps (30 kilometres) whilst men race 200 laps (50 kilometres). In the [[omnium]] points race, women race 80 laps (20 kilometres) whilst men race 100 laps (25 kilometres).

=== Water polo ===
The men's tournament contains twelve teams whereas the women's tournament contains ten teams. The women's tournament will also feature twelve teams from 2028.<ref>{{cite web|title=LA28 Medal Event program updates|url=https://la28.org/en/newsroom/los-angeles-2028-medal-event-program-and-athlete-quota.html|website=la28.org|publisher=Los Angeles Organizing Committee for the 2028 Olympic and Paralympic Games|access-date=April 9, 2025|date=|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20250409173954/https://la28.org/en/newsroom/los-angeles-2028-medal-event-program-and-athlete-quota.html|archive-date=April 9, 2025|url-status=live}}</ref> Men’s matches are played in a 30 metre pool, whereas women’s matches are played in a slightly shorter 25 metre pool, though both are 20 metres wide. Men also use a larger ball than the women.

=== Weightlifting ===
Men use a 20 kg [[barbell]], while women use a 15 kg barbell. Men's bars are 220 cm long with a 28mm diameter, whereas women's bars are 201 cm long with a 25 mm diameter. Men's bars feature centre knurling for better grip during cleans, while women's bars do not. The  weight classes for the 2028 Olympics will be 65 kg, 75 kg, 85 kg, 95 kg, 110 kg and +110 kg for the men and 53 kg, 61 kg, 69k g, 77 kg, 86 kg and +86 kg for the women.<ref>{{cite web|title=Bodyweight categories for the 2028 Olympics in Los Angeles are defined|url=https://iwf.sport/2025/11/03/bodyweight-categories-for-the-2028-olympics-in-los-angeles-are-defined/|website=iwf.sport|publisher=International Weightlifting Federation|access-date=February 15, 2026}}</ref>

== Gender equality ==
[[File:Flor Isava-Fonseca (cropped).jpg|thumb|In 1981 [[Flor Isava Fonseca]] and Finnish [[Pirjo Häggman]] were the first women to be elected to the [[International Olympic Committee]].<ref>[http://www.olympic.org/Documents/Reference_documents_Factsheets/The_Olympic_Movement.pdf International Olympic Committee Factsheet, June 2012] Retrieved 9 July 2013.</ref> She was the first woman to serve on the executive board in 1990.<ref>{{cite book|last=Findling, John E.|title=Encyclopedia of the modern Olympic movement|url=https://books.google.com/books?id=QmXi_-Jujj0C&dq=Flor+Isava-Fonseca+first+woman&pg=PA441|author2=Pelle, Kimberly|page=441|year=2002|publisher=Bloomsbury Academic |isbn=9780313322785}}</ref>]]

Historically, female athletes have been treated, portrayed and looked upon differently from their male counterparts. As the 20th century was coming to an end, Georgia state legislators suggested the Equity in Sports Act (House Bill 1308). Not only was this bill presented to strengthen the Title IX policies but also bring awareness to women sports. Later on in 2004, California passed the Fair Play Act which required gender equity, opportunities, facilities, and more.<ref>{{Cite web |title=Georgia's Equity in Sports Act 2000 – Equal Playing Fields |url=https://exhibits.library.gsu.edu/equal-playing-fields/georgias-equity-in-sports-act-2000/ |access-date=2024-06-11 |language=en}}</ref> As of 2024, more than 3 million girls participate in high school sports, compared to fewer than 300,000 girls before the law was passed.<ref>{{Cite web |title=HOME |url=https://www.fairplayforgirlsinsports.org/ |access-date=2024-06-11 |website=Fair Play Website |language=en}}</ref>

In the early days of the Olympic Games, many [[National Olympic Committee|NOCs]] sent fewer female competitors because they would incur the cost of a chaperone, which was not necessary for the male athletes.<ref>{{Cite news|url=https://www.nytimes.com/1996/06/23/magazine/how-the-women-won.html|title=How the Women Won|last=Longman|first=Jere|date=June 23, 1996|work=The New York Times|access-date=February 2, 2018|issn=0362-4331}}</ref> Female athletes continue to be treated as less than compared to male athletes.<ref>{{Cite news|url=https://www.telegraph.co.uk/news/worldnews/asia/japan/9407484/London-2012-Olympics-sexism-row-as-Japans-female-athletes-fly-lower-class.html|title=London 2012 Olympics: sexism row as Japan's female athletes fly lower class|last=Ryall|first=Julian|date=July 18, 2012|work=The Telegraph|access-date=February 2, 2018|issn=0307-1235}}</ref> For example, in 2012, the [[Japan women's national football team|Japan women's national football team]] travelled to the Games in economy class, while the men's team travelled in business class.<ref>{{Cite web|url=https://www.theguardian.com/world/2012/jul/19/japan-female-atheletes-economy-class|title=Japan's female athletes fly economy while men's team sit in business|last=McCurry|first=Justin|date=July 19, 2012|website=The Guardian|access-date=February 2, 2018}}</ref> Although women competed in all sports at the summer Olympics by 2012, there were still 39 events that were not open to women.<ref>{{Cite web|url=https://kpe.utoronto.ca/sites/default/files/donnelly-donnelly---olympic-gender-equality-report.pdf|title=The London 2012 Olympics: A Gender Equality Audit|last1=Donnelly|first1=Peter|last2=Donnelly|first2=Michele K.|date=September 2013|website=utoronto.ca|access-date=November 9, 2017|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20170101045740/https://kpe.utoronto.ca/sites/default/files/donnelly-donnelly---olympic-gender-equality-report.pdf|archive-date=January 1, 2017|url-status=live}}</ref> Men have to compete in longer and tougher events, such as the road race in cycling. A study done by "Women in International Elite Athletics: Gender (in)equality and National Participation",<ref>Meier, Henk E., et al. "Women in International Elite Athletics: Gender (in)equality and National Participation." ''Frontiers'', 4 August 2021, https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fspor.2021.709640/full . Accessed 6 February 2023.</ref> tested the effect of macro-social gender equality on the ability of women to pursue sports. There was also the idea that if the World Sports organisation made more pushes for equality campaigns, there would be a correlation in the number of women who play these sports.

== Sports Commentators ==
[[File:Andrea_Joyce_Delivers_the_Frank_Deford_Lecture_in_Sports_Journalism_(40578156403).jpg|left|thumb|[[Andrea Joyce]] has covered numerous Olympic Games for NBC Sports.]]

Historically, coverage and inclusion of women's team sports in the Olympics has been limited. Commentators are more likely to refer to female athletes using "non-sporting terminology" than they are for men.<ref>{{Cite news|url=http://www.cnn.com/2016/08/03/sport/sexism-sport-rio-olympic-games/index.html|title=Gender divides in the language of sport|last=Young|first=Henry|date=August 3, 2016|work=CNN|access-date=October 25, 2017}}</ref> A 2016 study published by [[Cambridge University Press]] found that women were more likely to be described using physical features, age, marital status and aesthetics than men were, as opposed to sport-related adjectives and descriptions.<ref>{{Cite news|url=http://www.cam.ac.uk/research/news/aesthetics-over-athletics-when-it-comes-to-women-in-sport|title=Aesthetics over athletics when it comes to women in sport|date=August 12, 2016|publisher=University of Cambridge|access-date=October 25, 2017}}</ref> The same study found that women were also more likely to be referred to as "girls" than men were to be called "boys" in commentary. In addition, women were twice as likely to be referred to as 'ladies', compared to 'gentlemen' who are frequently referred to by the neutral term 'men'.<ref name="cambridge.org">{{Cite news |title=Aesthetics, athletics and the olympics |date=August 5, 2016 |url=http://www.cambridge.org/about-us/news/aest/ |url-status=deviated |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20160811172824/http://www.cambridge.org/about-us/news/aest/ |archive-date=August 11, 2016 |publisher=[[Cambridge University Press]]}}</ref> This disparity in the quality of coverage for women's Olympic sports has been attributed to the fact that 90% of sports journalists are male.<ref>{{Cite web|url=https://www.poynter.org/news/media-failing-women-sports-journalism-particularly-so|title=Media is 'failing women' – sports journalism particularly so|last=Morrison|first=Sara|date=February 19, 2014|website=Poynter|access-date=October 28, 2017}}</ref>

The most common adjectives sports commentators used to describe returning female Olympians during the 2016 Olympics were 'aged', 'older', 'pregnant', and 'married' or 'un-married', while for male Olympians, 'fastest', 'strong', 'big', 'real' and 'great' were the most common adjectives.<ref name="cambridge.org"/>

Coverage of women's sports has typically been lower than men's. From 1992 to 1998, American women have always had less raw clock time when being covered on television. Compared to American men, the women have only had 44, 47, and 40 percent of the Olympic television coverage, respectively.<ref>{{Cite journal|last1=Billings|first1=Andrew C.|last2=Eastman|first2=Susan Tyler|date=2002-12-01|title=Selective Representation of Gender, Ethnicity, and Nationality in American Television Coverage of the 2000 Summer Olympics|url=https://doi.org/10.1177/101269020203700302|journal=International Review for the Sociology of Sport|language=en|volume=37|issue=3–4|pages=351–370|doi=10.1177/101269020203700302|s2cid=144331744|issn=1012-6902|url-access=subscription}}</ref>

== Role of the International Olympic Committee==
[[File:International Olympic Committee logo 2021.svg|thumbnail|International Olympic Committee logo.]]
The [[International Olympic Committee]] (IOC) was created by [[Pierre, Baron de Coubertin]], in 1894 and is now considered "the supreme authority of the Olympic movement".<ref name="olympic_a">{{cite web|title=IOC: The Organisation|url=http://www.olympic.org/about-ioc-institution|publisher=Olympic Movement|access-date=October 14, 2012}}</ref> Its headquarters are located in [[Lausanne, Switzerland]]. The title of supreme authority of the Olympic movement consists of many different duties, which include promoting Olympic values, maintaining the regular celebration of the [[Olympic Games]], and supporting any organization that is connected with the Olympic movement.<ref name="olympic_a" />

Some of the Olympic values that the IOC promotes are practicing sport ethically, eliminating discrimination from sports, encouraging women's involvement in sport, fighting the use of drugs in sport, and blending sport, culture, and education.<ref name="olympic_a" /> The IOC supports these values by creating different commissions that focus on a particular area. These commissions hold conferences throughout the year where different people around the world discuss ideas and ways to implement the Olympic values into the lives of people internationally.<ref name="olympic_a" /> The commissions also have the responsibility of reporting their findings to the [[President of the International Olympic Committee|President of the IOC]] and its executive board.<ref name="olympic_a" /> The President has the authority to assign members to different commissions based on the person's interests and specialties.

The first two female IOC members were the Venezuelan [[Flor Isava-Fonseca]] and the Finnish [[Pirjo Häggman]] and were co-opted as IOC members in 1981.<ref>{{Cite journal|date=April 2013|title=Women in the 2012 Olympic and Paralympic Games: An Analysis of Participation and Leadership Opportunities|url=http://sharp.research.umich.edu/wp-content/uploads/2017/03/olympic_report_2012_final-4-11-13.pdf|journal=SHARP Center for Women and Girls|pages=1–76|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20191221141026/http://sharp.research.umich.edu/wp-content/uploads/2017/03/olympic_report_2012_final-4-11-13.pdf|archive-date=December 21, 2019|url-status=dead}}</ref>

The IOC can contain up to 115 members, and currently, the members of the IOC come from 79 countries.<ref name="olympic_a" /> The IOC is considered a powerful authority throughout the world as it creates policies that become standards for other countries to follow in the sporting arena.<ref name="Divisionfor">{{cite journal|last=Division for the Advancement of Women of the United Nations Secretariat|title=Women, Gender Equality and Sport|journal=Women 2000 and Beyond|date=December 2007|pages=2–40|url=http://www.un.org/womenwatch/daw/public/Women%20and%20Sport.pdf|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20080413003805/http://www.un.org/womenwatch/daw/public/Women%20and%20Sport.pdf|archive-date=April 13, 2008|url-status=live}}</ref>

In 2011 only 20 of the 106 members of the IOC were women. In 2023, this was 44 of 107 members - 41.1%.<ref name="IOCWomen">{{cite web |url=http://www.olympic.org/Documents/Reference_documents_Factsheets/Women_in_Olympic_Movement.pdf |title=Women in the Olympic movement |publisher=International Olympic Committee |access-date=December 29, 2012 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20110805134605/http://www.olympic.org/Documents/Reference_documents_Factsheets/Women_in_Olympic_Movement.pdf |archive-date=August 5, 2011 |url-status=dead }}</ref><ref name="IOC female representation 2023">{{cite web |url=https://olympics.com/ioc/news/ioc-member-elections-lead-to-increased-female-representation-among-the-membership |title=IOC Member elections lead to increased female representation among the membership |publisher=International Olympic Committee |access-date=July 28, 2024}}</ref>

=== Women in Sport Commission ===
[[File:Women percent summer olympics.png|thumb|Women participants at each [[Summer Olympic Games]] as a percentage of all participants.|left]]
A goal of the IOC is to encourage these traditional countries to support women's participation in sport because two of the IOC's Olympic values that it must uphold are ensuring the lack of discrimination in sports and promoting women's involvement in sport. The commission that was created to promote the combination of these values was the [[International Olympic Committee#IOC Commissions|Women in Sport Commission]].<ref name=":9">{{Cite news|url=https://www.olympic.org/women-in-sport-commission|title=Women in Sport Commission|date=October 27, 2017|publisher=International Olympic Committee|access-date=January 7, 2018}}</ref> This commission declares its role as "advis[ing] the IOC Executive Board on the policy to deploy in the area of promoting women in sport".<ref name=":9" /> This commission did not become fully promoted to its status until 2004, and it meets once a year to discuss its goals and implementations.<ref name=":9" /> This commission also presents a Women and Sport Trophy annually which recognizes a woman internationally who has embodied the values of the IOC and who has supported efforts to increase women's participation in sport at all levels.<ref name="Divisionfor" /> This trophy is supposed to symbolize the IOC's commitment to honoring those who are beneficial to gender equality in sports.<ref>{{Cite web|url=http://www.aipsmedia.com/2017/11/10/21949/women-in-sport-ioc-international-olympic-committee-coaching-lifetime-achievement|title=IOC celebrates the power of Women in Sport, honors coaches in new Lifetime Achievement Awards|date=November 11, 2017|website=International Sports Press Association|access-date=January 7, 2018}}</ref>

Another way that the IOC tried to support women's participation in sport was allowing women to become members. In 1990, [[Flor Isava Fonseca]] became the first woman elected to the executive board of the IOC. The first American woman member of the IOC was [[Anita DeFrantz]], who became a member in 1986<ref name="SharpKathleen">{{cite journal|last=Sharp|first=Kathleen|title=Unsung Heroes|journal=Women's Sports and Fitness|year=1996|volume=18|issue=5|pages=64}}</ref> and in 1992 began chairing the prototype of the [[Softball at the Summer Olympics#Inclusion on the Olympic programme|IOC Commission on Women in Sport]]. DeFrantz not only worked towards promoting gender equality in sports, but she also wanted to move toward gender equality in the IOC so women could be equally represented. She believed that without equal representation in the IOC that women's voices would not get an equal chance to be heard. She was instrumental in creating a new IOC policy that required the IOC membership to be composed of at least 20 percent women by 2005.<ref name="SharpKathleen" /> She also commissioned a study conducted in 1989 and again in 1994 that focused on the difference between televised coverage of men's and women's sports.<ref name="SharpKathleen" /> [[Gender inequality|Inequality]] still exists in this area, but her study was deemed to be eye opening to how substantial the problem was and suggested ways to increase reporting on women's sporting events. DeFrantz is now head of the Women in Sport Commission.

The IOC failed in its policy requiring 20 percent of IOC members to be women by 2005.<ref name="IOCWomen"/> By June 2012, the policy had still not been achieved, with only 20 out of 106 IOC members women, an 18.8 percent ratio. Only 4 percent of [[National Olympic Committee]]s have female presidents.<ref name="IOCWomen"/>

== Impact of the Women's World Games ==
{{Main|Women's World Games}}

=== Background ===
[[File:Alice milliat.jpg|thumb|right|Alice Milliat, the founder of the IWSF and Women's World Games.]]
In 1919, French translator and amateur rower, [[Alice Milliat]] initiated talks with the IOC and [[International Association of Athletics Federations]] with the goal of having women's athletics included at the 1924 Summer Olympics.<ref>{{Cite web|url=http://library.la84.org/OlympicInformationCenter/OlympicReview/2000/OREXXVI31/OREXXVI31zb.pdf|title=Alice Milliat and the Women's Games|last=Quintillan|first=Ghislaine|website=Olympic Review|issn=0251-3498|access-date=August 3, 2017|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20131213110343/http://library.la84.org/OlympicInformationCenter/OlympicReview/2000/OREXXVI31/OREXXVI31zb.pdf|archive-date=December 13, 2013|url-status=dead}}</ref> After her request was refused, she organised the first "[[1921 Women's Olympiad|Women's Olympiad]]", hosted in [[Monte Carlo]].<ref>{{Cite web|url=https://www.vice.com/en/article/throwback-thursday-how-a-french-feminist-staged-her-own-games-and-forced-the-olympics-to-include-women/|title=Throwback Thursday: How a French Feminist Staged Her Own Games and Forced the Olympics to Include Women|last=Blickenstaff|first=Brian|date=August 11, 2017|website=Vice Sports|access-date=August 3, 2017}}</ref> This would become the precursor to the first Women's World Games. The event was seen as a protest against the IOC's refusal to include females in athletics and a message to their President [[Pierre de Coubertin]] who was opposed to women at the Olympics.<ref>{{Cite web|url=http://www.caaws-womenatthegames.ca/olympics/2004/history/womens_games.cfm|title=The Women's Olympic Games: Important Breakthrough Obscured By Time|last=Kidd|first=Bruce|date=Spring 1994|website=Canadian Association for the Advancement of Women and Sport and Physical Activity|access-date=August 3, 2017|archive-date=December 2, 2013|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20131202222439/http://www.caaws-womenatthegames.ca/olympics/2004/history/womens_games.cfm|url-status=dead}}</ref> Milliat went on to found the [[International Women's Sports Federation]] who organized the first Women's World Games.

=== The Games ===
[[File:RIAN archive 556180 Women's field hockey team from Zimbabwe.jpg|thumb|left|The [[Zimbabwe]]an women's field hockey team celebrating a 4–0 win over Poland at the 1980 Games.]]

The first ever "Women's Olympic Games" were held in [[1922 Women's World Games|Paris in 1922]]. The athletes competed in eleven events:<ref>{{Cite web|url=http://lits.columbiasc.edu/edenslibrary/jane/results.htm|title=Complete results of the First International Track Meet for Women|last=Tuttle|first=Jane|year=2002|website=They Set the Mark|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20131019150431/http://lits.columbiasc.edu/edenslibrary/jane/results.htm|archive-date=October 19, 2013|access-date=August 4, 2017}}</ref> [[60 metres]], [[100-yard dash|100 yards]], [[Sprint (running)#Historical and uncommon distances|300 metres]], [[Middle-distance running#1000 metres|1000 metres]], [[Relay race|4 x 110 yards relay]], [[Hurdling]] 100 yards, [[high jump]], [[long jump]], [[standing long jump]], [[javelin]] and [[shot put]]. 20,000 people attended the Games and 18 world records were set.<ref>{{Cite web|url=http://home.nordnet.fr/scharlet/feminin.htm|title=Chronique de l'athlétisme féminin|last=Sylvain|first=Charlet|date=November 3, 2008|website=home.nordnet.fr|language=fr|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20160303213730/http://home.nordnet.fr/scharlet/feminin.htm|archive-date=2016-03-03|url-status=dead|access-date=August 4, 2017}}</ref> Despite the successful outcome of the event, the IOC still refused to include women's athletics at the 1924 Summer Olympics. On top of this, the IOC and IAAF objected to the use of the term "Olympic" in the event, so the IWSF changed the name of the event to the Women's World Games for the 1926 version.<ref>{{Cite web|url=http://www.gbrathletics.com/ic/fsfi.htm|title=Women's World and European Games|website=gbrathletics|access-date=August 4, 2017}}</ref> The [[1926 Women's World Games]] would be held in Gothenburg, Sweden. The discus throw was added to the programme. These Games were also attended by 20,000 spectators and finally convinced the IOC to allow women to compete in the Olympics in some athletics events.<ref>{{Cite journal|last1=Parčina|first1=Ivana|last2=Šiljak|first2=Violeta|last3=Perović|first3=Aleksandra|last4=Plakona|first4=Elena|year=2014|title=Women's World Games|url=http://www.fiep-serbia.net/docs/vol-1-i-2/en/paper-5.pdf|journal=Physical Education and Sport Through the Centuries|pages=49–60|issn=2335-0660|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20160519011659/http://www.fiep-serbia.net/docs/vol-1-i-2/en/paper-5.pdf|archive-date=May 19, 2016|url-status=live}}</ref> The IOC let women compete in 100 metres, 800&nbsp;metres, 4 × 100&nbsp;metres relay, high jump and discus throw in 1928.<ref>{{Cite news|url=https://www.bitchmedia.org/article/forgotten-history-female-athletes-who-organized-their-own-olympics|title=The Forgotten History of Female Athletes Who Organized Their Own Olympics|last=Boykoff|first=Jules|date=July 26, 2016|work=Bitch Media|access-date=October 28, 2017|archive-date=April 5, 2019|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20190405004518/https://www.bitchmedia.org/article/forgotten-history-female-athletes-who-organized-their-own-olympics|url-status=dead}}</ref> There would be two more editions of the Women's World Games, [[1930 Women's World Games|1930]] in Prague and [[1934 Women's World Games|1934]] in London.<ref>{{Cite web|url=https://curiosity.com/topics/when-women-were-denied-from-the-olympics-a-womens-only-version-emerged-curiosity/|title=When Women Were Denied From The Olympics, A Women's-Only Version Emerged|website=Curiosity.com|access-date=October 29, 2017|archive-date=March 27, 2019|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20190327221824/https://curiosity.com/topics/when-women-were-denied-from-the-olympics-a-womens-only-version-emerged-curiosity/|url-status=dead}}</ref> The IWSF was forced to fold after the [[Government of France]] pulled funding in 1936.<ref>{{Cite web|url=http://www.wispsports.com/womens-sports-blogs/off-side-by-sara-gross/alice-milliat-womens-olympic-games/|title=Alice Milliat and the Women's Olympic Games|last=Goss|first=Sara|date=June 17, 2016|website=wispsports.com|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20160625052748/http://www.wispsports.com/womens-sports-blogs/off-side-by-sara-gross/alice-milliat-womens-olympic-games/|archive-date=2016-06-25|url-status=dead|access-date=October 29, 2017}}</ref> [[Pierre de Coubertin]], founder of the International Olympic Committee, was quoted with saying "I do not approve of the participation of women in public competitions. In the Olympic Games, their primary role should be to crown the victors."
