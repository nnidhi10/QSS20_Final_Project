The '''World Figure Skating Championships''' are an annual [[figure skating competition]] sanctioned by the [[International Skating Union]] (ISU). The World Championships are considered the most prestigious event in figure skating.<ref name=":0">{{Cite web |last=Murphy |first=Margot |date=March 31, 2025 |title=Victors made in the City of Champions: ISU World Figure Skating Championship returns to Boston |url=https://huntnewsnu.com/85501/city/victors-made-in-the-city-of-champions-isu-world-figure-skating-championship-returns-to-boston/ |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20250704143956/https://huntnewsnu.com/85501/city/victors-made-in-the-city-of-champions-isu-world-figure-skating-championship-returns-to-boston/ |archive-date=July 4, 2025 |access-date=November 15, 2025 |website=[[The Huntington News]]}}</ref> The first World Championships were held in 1896 in [[Saint Petersburg, Russia]], and they have been held ever since with only four interruptions. A separate competition for women was established in 1905, with the men's and women's events held as separate competitions for several years. [[Pair skating]] was added in 1908 and [[ice dance]] in 1952. Skaters are eligible to compete at the World Championships, provided they represent a [[International figure skating|member nation]] of the International Skating Union and are selected by their respective federation.   

The corresponding competition for junior-level skaters is the [[World Junior Figure Skating Championships]]. The corresponding competitions for [[synchronized skating]] are the [[ISU World Synchronized Skating Championships|World Synchronized Skating Championships]] and the [[ISU World Junior Synchronized Skating Championships|World Junior Synchronized Skating Championships]].

Medals are awarded in [[Single skating|men's singles, women's singles]], pair skating, and ice dance. [[Ulrich Salchow]] of Sweden holds the record for winning the most World Championship titles in men's singles (with ten), while [[Sonja Henie]] of Norway holds the record in women's singles (also with ten). [[Irina Rodnina]] and [[Alexander Zaitsev (pair skater)|Alexander Zaitsev]] of the Soviet Union hold the record in pair skating (with six), although Rodnina won an additional four titles with a previous partner. [[Lyudmila Pakhomova]] and [[Aleksandr Gorshkov (figure skater)|Aleksandr Gorshkov]] of the Soviet Union hold the record in ice dance (with six).

== History ==
The [[International Skating Union]] (ISU) was formed in 1892 to govern international competition in [[speed skating]] and figure skating.<ref name="ISUhistory" /> The first World Championship event in figure skating, known as the Championship of the {{lang|de|Internationale Eislauf-Vereingung}}, was held in [[Saint Petersburg, Russia]], in 1896. There were four competitors and the winner of the event was [[Gilbert Fuchs]] of Germany.<ref name="Hines2006">{{cite book |last=Hines |first=James R. |title=Figure Skating: A History |publisher=[[University of Illinois Press]] |year=2006 |isbn=9780252072864 |pages=76–83; 87–91 |language=en}}</ref>

Since competitive skating was generally viewed as a male sport, the championships were presumed to be an exclusive male event. However, there were no specific rules barring women from competing. In 1902, [[Madge Syers]] of Great Britain entered the championships and won the silver medal.<ref name="Hines2006" /> The [[International Skating Union#ISU Congress|ISU Congress]] – the highest-ranking decision-making body of the ISU – considered gender issues at their meeting in 1903, but passed no new rules. The 1905 ISU Congress established a second-class women's competition called the ISU Championships, rather than World Championships; its winners were known as ISU Champions and not World Champions.<ref name="Hines2006" /> Men's and women's events were generally held separately. The first competition for women was held in [[Davos|Davos, Switzerland]], in 1906;<ref name="ISUhistory" /> the event was won by Syers.<ref name="Hines2006" />

The first competition for [[pair skating]] was held in Saint Petersburg in 1908.<ref name="Hines2006" /> Early championships for both women and pairs – previously called ISU Championships – were retroactively given World Championship status in 1924.<ref name="ISUhistory" />

In the early years, judges were invited by the host country and were often native to that country. At the [[1927 World Figure Skating Championships|1927]] women's event in [[Oslo|Oslo, Norway]], three of the five judges were Norwegian; those three judges awarded first place to Norwegian competitor [[Sonja Henie]], while the Austrian and German judges placed defending champion [[Herma Szabo]] of Austria first. The controversial result stood, awarding Henie her first World Championship title, but the controversy led to the ISU introducing a new rule that allowed no more than one judge per country on a panel.<ref name=Hines2015>{{cite book |last=Hines |first=James R. |title=Figure Skating in the Formative Years: Singles, Pairs, and the Expanding Role of Women |publisher=University of Illinois Press |year=2015 |isbn=9780252039065 |pages=80–81 |language=en}}</ref>

The [[1930 World Figure Skating Championships|1930 World Championships]] in [[New York City]] were the first to be held outside Europe and combined all three competitions – men's singles, women's singles, and pair skating – into one event for the first time.<ref name="ISUhistory">{{cite web |title=Some Key Dates in ISU History |url=http://www.isu.org/vsite/vcontent/page/custom/0,8510,4844-130844-132152-20256-74409-custom-item,00.html |url-status=dead |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20100722191739/http://www.isu.org/vsite/vcontent/page/custom/0,8510,4844-130844-132152-20256-74409-custom-item,00.html |archive-date=July 22, 2010 |work=[[International Skating Union]]}}</ref> [[Ice dance]] made its debut at the [[1952 World Figure Skating Championships|1952 World Championships]].<ref name=ISUhistory/> [[Compulsory figures]] were retired from the World Championships after [[1990 World Figure Skating Championships|1990]].<ref>{{cite magazine |last=Johnson |first=Susan A. |date=April 1991 |title=And Then There Were None |url=https://magazinearchive.blob.core.windows.net/resources/issue/199104.pdf |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20250302153742/https://magazinearchive.blob.core.windows.net/resources/issue/199104.pdf |archive-date=March 2, 2025 |access-date=March 1, 2025 |magazine=[[Skating (magazine)|Skating]] |pages=11–16 |volume=68 |issue=4}}</ref>

As the [[Figure skating at the Winter Olympics|Winter Olympics]] are usually held in February, every four years the World Championships occur roughly a month after Olympic events have ended. Historically, a number of Olympic medalists have chosen to skip the following World Championships; many skaters need time to rest due to physical and mental exhaustion, and some Olympic medalists chose to capitalize on their success by becoming professional skaters with companies such as the [[Ice Capades]] or [[Champions on Ice]].<ref name="AP100329">{{cite news |last=Barry |first=Colleen |date=March 29, 2010 |title=Olympic-Fatigued Skaters Bumble Through Worlds |url=https://www.google.com/hostednews/ap/article/ALeqM5jaV3nJwPlFS4UsxX1EjQfRQuVt7AD9ENP7D80 |url-status=dead |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20100331131738/http://www.google.com/hostednews/ap/article/ALeqM5jaV3nJwPlFS4UsxX1EjQfRQuVt7AD9ENP7D80 |archive-date=March 31, 2010 |work= |agency=[[Associated Press]]}}</ref> Prior to 1993, professional skaters were barred from competing at the Olympics by the ISU, so choosing to pursue skating professionally had once meant the end of one's competitive career.<ref>{{Cite web |last=Harvey |first=Randy |date=March 30, 1993 |title=Boitano Plans to Skate in '94 Olympics: Winter Games: The 1988 gold medalist, who had turned pro, will apply to regain his eligibility for the competition at Lillehammer, Norway |url=https://www.latimes.com/archives/la-xpm-1993-03-30-sp-16946-story.html |url-access=subscription |access-date=May 9, 2023 |website=[[Los Angeles Times]]}}</ref>

The World Championships have been interrupted four times in the competition's history: from 1915 through 1921 due to [[World War I]],<ref name="Hines2006" /> from 1940 through 1946 due to [[World War II]],<ref name="Hines2006" /> in 1961 after the crash of [[Sabena Flight 548]],<ref name="skcnc">{{cite news |date=February 16, 1961 |title=Skating Cancelled |url=https://news.google.com/newspapers?id=nv0xAAAAIBAJ&pg=1436%2C3351165 |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20250131082726/https://news.google.com/newspapers?pg=1436%2C3351165&id=nv0xAAAAIBAJ |archive-date=January 31, 2025 |access-date=February 9, 2025 |newspaper=[[Ottawa Citizen]] |page=1 |via=[[Google News Archive]] |agency=[[Associated Press]]}}</ref> and in 2020 due to the [[Impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on sports#Figure skating|COVID-19 pandemic]].<ref>{{Cite web |date=March 11, 2020 |title=World Figure Skating Championships Canceled Due to Coronavirus, At Least for Now |url=https://www.nbcsports.com/olympics/news/figure-skating-world-championships-coronavirus-canceled |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20250302154509/https://www.nbcsports.com/olympics/news/figure-skating-world-championships-coronavirus-canceled |archive-date=March 2, 2025 |access-date=February 9, 2025 |website=[[NBC Sports]]}}</ref>

The [[2026 World Figure Skating Championships|2026 World Championships]] are scheduled to be held from March 23 to 29 in [[Prague|Prague, Czech Republic]],<ref>{{Cite web |date= |title=2026 World Figure Skating Championships |url=https://www.goldenskate.com/events/world-figure-skating-championships-2026/ |access-date=February 12, 2025 |website=Golden Skate |language=en-US |archive-date=November 9, 2024 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20241109034214/https://www.goldenskate.com/events/world-figure-skating-championships-2026/ |url-status=live }}</ref> while the 2027 World Championships are scheduled to be held from March 15 to 21 in [[Tampere, Finland]].<ref>{{Cite web |date=April 10, 2025 |title=World Figure Skating Championships arrive in Tampere |url=https://www.tampere.fi/en/current/2025/04/10/world-figure-skating-championships-arrive-tampere#:~:text=The%20World%20Figure%20Skating%20Championships,top-level%20sport%20to%20Finland. |access-date=April 10, 2025 |website=[[Tampere|Tampere.Finland]] |language=en}}</ref>

==Qualifying==
Skaters may compete at the World Championships if they represent a [[International figure skating|member nation]] of the [[International Skating Union]] (ISU) and are selected by their federation. Member nations select their entries according to their own criteria. Some countries rely on the results of their national championships, while others have more varied criteria, which may include success at certain international events or specific technical requirements. All of the selected skaters must have earned the minimum [[ISU judging system#Technical Element Score (TES)|total element scores]], which are determined and published each season by the ISU, during the current or immediately previous season. Member nations may enter at least one competitor or team in each discipline; while a points system allows nations to enter additional competitors or teams, up to a total of three per discipline, based on the nation's performance in that discipline at the previous World Championships.<ref name="ISURules">{{Cite web |date=2022 |title=Special Regulations & Technical Rules |url=https://www.isu.org/figure-skating/rules/fsk-regulations-rules/file |url-status=dead |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20240220124349/https://www.isu.org/figure-skating/rules/fsk-regulations-rules/file |archive-date=February 20, 2024 |website=[[International Skating Union]]}}</ref>

Age restrictions have changed throughout the history of the World Championships. Originally there were no age restrictions. For example, [[Sonja Henie]] of Norway – a three-time Olympic champion and ten-time World Champion in women's singles – debuted at the World Championships in [[1924 World Figure Skating Championships|1924]] at the age of 11.<ref name="Hines2015" /> Beginning with the 1996–97 season, skaters had to be at least 15 years old before July 1 of the previous year.<ref name="espn">{{cite news |date=February 14, 2011 |title=China Eyed over Eight Athletes' Ages |url=https://www.espn.com/olympics/figureskating/news/story?id=6120559 |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20110629003445/http://sports.espn.go.com/oly/figureskating/news/story?id=6120559 |archive-date=June 29, 2011 |work=[[ESPN]] |agency=[[Associated Press]]}}</ref> However, the ISU allowed for two exceptions. Firstly, skaters younger than 15 who had already competed in senior-level international events could continue. For example, [[Tara Lipinski]] of the United States – the [[Figure skating at the 1998 Winter Olympics|1998 Olympic]] champion in women's singles who debuted at the World Championships in [[1996 World Figure Skating Championships|1996]] at the age of 13 – was allowed to participate at the [[1997 World Figure Skating Championships|1997 World Championships]], where she won a gold medal at the age of 14.<ref>{{Cite news |last=Longman |first=Jere |date=March 23, 1997 |title=Lipinski, 14, Is Youngest World Champion |url=https://www.nytimes.com/1997/03/23/sports/lipinski-14-is-youngest-world-champion.html |url-access=subscription |url-status=live |work=[[The New York Times]] |language=en-US |issn=0362-4331}}</ref> That exception expired naturally after a couple of seasons.<ref>{{cite news |last=Loosemore |first=Sandra |date=June 15, 1998 who had won medals at the [[World Junior Figure Skating Championships]] were permitted to compete as seniors at the ISU Championships.<ref name="cbs000316" /> For example, [[Sarah Hughes]] of the United States – the [[Figure skating at the 2002 Winter Olympics|2002 Olympic]] champion in women's singles – won the silver medal at the [[1999 World Junior Figure Skating Championships|1999 World Junior Championships]], and was thus allowed to participate at the [[1999 World Figure Skating Championships|1999 World Championships]] at the age of 13.<ref name="cbs000316">{{cite news |last=Loosemore |first=Sandra |date=March 16, 2000 |title=Junior Skaters Shouldn't Face Senior Pressure |url=http://cbs.sportsline.com/u/ce/multi/0,1329,2150632_10946,00.html |url-status=unfit |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20081013030332/http://cbs.sportsline.com/u/ce/multi/0,1329,2150632_10946,00.html |archive-date=October 13, 2008 |work=[[CBS Sportsline]]}}</ref> That exception lasted through the 1999–2000 season.<ref>{{cite news |last=Hersh |first=Philip |date=May 17, 2018 |title=Proposal to raise minimum age for senior events brings figure skating back to the future At the [[International Skating Union#ISU Congress|ISU Congress]] held in June 2022, members of the [[International Skating Union#ISU Council|ISU Council]] – the ISU's executive body responsible for determining policies – accepted a proposal to gradually increase the minimum age limit for senior competition to 17 beginning with the 2024–25 season. To avoid forcing skaters who had already competed in the senior category to return to juniors, the age limit remained unchanged during the 2022–23 season, before increasing to 16 during the 2023–24 season, and then to 17 during the 2024–25 season.<ref>{{cite web |url=https://www.reuters.com/lifestyle/sports/isu-raise-mininum-age-senior-competitions-17-following-doping-saga-2022-06-07/ |title=ISU to Raise Minimum Age for Senior Competitions to 17 |date=June 7, 2022 |website=[[Reuters]] |access-date=June 11, 2022 |archive-date=June 10, 2022 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20220610095530/https://www.reuters.com/lifestyle/sports/isu-raise-mininum-age-senior-competitions-17-following-doping-saga-2022-06-07/ |url-status=live}}</ref>
